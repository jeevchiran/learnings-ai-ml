<Bridge>
**Factory QC.** A cereal line targets 500 g per box, with a long-established known $\sigma = 8$ g. After maintenance, the manager samples 40 boxes and gets $\bar{x} = 497$ g. Is the 3 g shortfall random variation or systematic underfilling? To decide with a controlled error risk, she needs the **one-sample Z-test**.
</Bridge>

<ConceptBox title="TL;DR">
- Four conditions, and the second is the one that decides everything: **$\sigma$ known** → Z-test; $\sigma$ estimated from the sample → t-test.
- The **sampling distribution** describes how $\bar{x}$ moves across hypothetical samples — not how the data are spread. Its width is $\text{SE}=\sigma/\sqrt{n}$.
- $Z=(\bar{x}-\mu_0)/\text{SE}$ reads as "observed minus predicted, in units of ordinary sampling wobble."
- The CLT is what lets you use it on non-normal populations once $n\gtrsim30$.
- Same data, different $\alpha$, different verdict — which is exactly why $\alpha$ is fixed first.
</ConceptBox>

## When to Use It

Compare *one* observed sample mean against a claimed population mean $\mu_0$. Four conditions must all hold:

<ConceptBox title="The four conditions">
1. **Single sample vs. a claimed value** — one group against a target, not two groups compared.
2. **Known population $\sigma$** — from prior study or long-run process data. If $\sigma$ is unknown and estimated from the sample, use the t-test instead.
3. **Random, independent observations** — knowing one value tells you nothing about another.
4. **Approximate normality of $\bar{x}$** — either the population is normal, or $n \geq 30$ so the **Central Limit Theorem** makes $\bar{x}$ approximately normal regardless of population shape.

In the factory case: $\sigma = 8$ known ✓, $n = 40 \geq 30$ ✓, random ✓ — all satisfied.
</ConceptBox>

## The Sampling Distribution of the Mean

This describes how $\bar{x}$ behaves across all possible samples of size $n$, not the data itself. If $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, then

$$\bar{X} \sim N\!\left(\mu,\, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right), \qquad \text{SE}(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$

The **standard error** is how much $\bar{x}$ typically varies sample-to-sample by chance. It shrinks with $n$ — larger samples estimate $\mu$ more precisely. One box varies by 8 g, but the mean of 40 varies by only $8/\sqrt{40} \approx 1.265$ g.

## Deriving the Z Statistic

<DerivationSteps>
<DerivationStep number={1} title="Null distribution">
Under $H_0$ ($\mu = \mu_0$): $\bar{X} \sim N(\mu_0,\, \sigma^2/n)$ — all probabilities are computed relative to this.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={2} title="Standardize">
Subtract the mean, divide by the SD:
$$Z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu_0}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}}$$
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={3} title="Null distribution of Z">
$$Z \sim N(0,1) \quad \text{under } H_0$$
Exactly (normal population) or approximately (CLT, large $n$) — one universal, fully tabulated reference.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={4} title="Interpret magnitude">
$Z$ is in standard-error units. $Z = 2$ means $\bar{x}$ is 2 SEs from $\mu_0$ — an event happening ~4.6% of the time under $H_0$. Large $|Z|$ = the sample mean would be very unlikely if $H_0$ held.
</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

Each part carries meaning: **numerator** $\bar{x} - \mu_0$ = observed minus predicted (497 − 500 = −3 g); **denominator** $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ = typical sampling movement; **ratio** = the gap in units of natural variation. The further $|Z|$ from 0, the smaller the p-value.

## The Testing Procedure

<DerivationSteps>
<DerivationStep number={1} title="State hypotheses">
$H_0: \mu = \mu_0$; $H_1$ gives direction ($\neq$, $<$, or $>$) from the research question.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={2} title="Choose α, verify assumptions">
Pick $\alpha$ (0.05, 0.01) *before* computing anything, and confirm all four conditions — applying the Z-test with unknown $\sigma$ or small $n$ gives invalid p-values.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={3} title="Compute the statistic">
$$z_{\text{obs}} = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}}$$
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={4} title="p-value or critical value">
Left: $p = \Phi(z)$. Right: $p = 1-\Phi(z)$. Two-tailed: $p = 2\,\Phi(-|z|)$. Reject if $p < \alpha$. Critical values: $\pm1.96$ (two-tailed, 0.05), $\pm2.576$ (0.01).
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={5} title="Decide">
**Reject** or **fail to reject** (never "accept").
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={6} title="Conclude in context">
Translate back — significance level, direction, practical implication.
</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

## Confidence Interval Duality

A two-tailed Z-test rejects $H_0: \mu = \mu_0$ iff $\mu_0$ falls *outside* the $(1-\alpha)$ CI for $\mu$:

$$\bar{X} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$

Factory ($\bar{x} = 497$, $\sigma = 8$, $n = 40$): $497 \pm 1.96 \times \frac{8}{\sqrt{40}} = 497 \pm 2.48 = [494.52, 499.48]$. Since 500 is outside, reject at 5%. The CI also shows *which* means are plausible — more informative than a bare reject/fail decision.

## Worked Example: The Factory

$\mu_0 = 500$, $\bar{x} = 497$, $\sigma = 8$, $n = 40$, two-tailed, $\alpha = 0.05$.

<DerivationSteps>
<DerivationStep number={1} title="Hypotheses">
$H_0: \mu = 500$ vs $H_1: \mu \neq 500$ — two-tailed (off in either direction is a fault).
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={2} title="α and assumptions">
$\alpha = 0.05$. $\sigma$ known ✓, $n = 40 \geq 30$ ✓, random ✓.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={3} title="Statistic">
$$\text{SE} = \frac{8}{\sqrt{40}} \approx 1.265, \qquad z_{\text{obs}} = \frac{497 - 500}{1.265} \approx -2.372$$
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={4} title="p-value and critical value">
$p = 2\,\Phi(-2.372) \approx 0.0176 < 0.05$ → reject. Critical: $|z| = 2.372 > 1.96$ → reject. Agree.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={5} title="Decision">
**Reject $H_0$ at $\alpha = 0.05$.**
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={6} title="Conclusion">
At 5%, the mean output differs from 500 g; the 95% CI [494.52, 499.48] excludes 500, confirming it. Halt and recalibrate.
</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

## Practice

A city claims a highway commute averages 25 min. A group times 64 trips, gets $\bar{x} = 27.4$ min; historical $\sigma = 9.6$ min. Test at $\alpha = 0.01$, both methods.

<ConceptBox title="Solution">
**Hypotheses:** $H_0: \mu = 25$ vs $H_1: \mu \neq 25$ (two-tailed). $\alpha = 0.01$; conditions met.

**Statistic:** $\text{SE} = 9.6/\sqrt{64} = 1.2$; $z_{\text{obs}} = (27.4 - 25)/1.2 = 2.00$.

**p-value:** $2 \times P(Z > 2.00) = 0.0456 > 0.01$ → **fail to reject**.

**Critical value:** $z^* = 2.576$; $2.00 < 2.576$ → **fail to reject**. Agree.

**Conclusion:** At 1%, insufficient evidence the mean differs from 25 min. Note: at $\alpha = 0.05$ we'd have rejected ($0.0456 < 0.05$) — exactly why $\alpha$ is fixed *before* data.
</ConceptBox>
