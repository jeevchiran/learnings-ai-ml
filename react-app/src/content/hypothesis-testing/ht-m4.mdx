## The Two-Sample Framework

Module 3 compared one mean $\bar{x}$ to a fixed $\mu_0$. Two-sample tests compare the means of **two independent populations** directly — the parameter of interest is the difference $\mu_1 - \mu_2$:

$$H_0 : \mu_1 - \mu_2 = \delta_0 \;\; (\text{usually } 0) \qquad H_1 : \mu_1 - \mu_2 \neq \delta_0 \;\;(\text{or } >, <)$$

Same principle as before — estimator minus null, over standard error:

$$\text{Test statistic} = \frac{(\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2) - \delta_0}{\text{SE}(\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2)}$$

<ConceptBox title="Independence assumption">
The two groups must be **independent** — knowing any value in group 1 tells you nothing about group 2 (guaranteed by random assignment). If the same subjects appear in both (before/after, matched pairs), the two-sample test is invalid — use the **paired t-test** (Module 5).
</ConceptBox>

## Two-Population Z-Test (Known σ)

With both $\sigma_1, \sigma_2$ known, the analysis is exact. By the variance-addition rule for independent normals:

$$\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2 \sim N\!\left(\mu_1 - \mu_2,\; \frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}\right), \qquad Z = \frac{(\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2) - \delta_0}{\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}}}$$

Under $H_0$, $Z \sim N(0,1)$. Population SDs are almost never known, so this is mainly a stepping stone to the t-test.

## Two-Sample Independent t-Test (Unknown σ)

Substitute sample SDs $s_1, s_2$. The added uncertainty means the statistic follows approximately a Student's t:

$$t = \frac{(\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2) - \delta_0}{\sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2}}}$$

The **Welch-Satterthwaite** effective degrees of freedom:

$$\nu \approx \frac{\left(\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2}\right)^2}{\frac{(s_1^2/n_1)^2}{n_1 - 1} + \frac{(s_2^2/n_2)^2}{n_2 - 1}}$$

Round $\nu$ down before looking up critical values; it lies between $\min(n_1,n_2)-1$ and $n_1+n_2-2$.

<ConceptBox title="Welch vs. pooled">
The older **pooled** t-test assumes equal population variances — invalid when they differ, which is common. **Welch's** test doesn't assume equal variances and keeps correct Type I error rates even when they differ substantially. Modern practice: **Welch is the default.** As $n_1, n_2 \to \infty$, t approaches the standard normal and the t-test converges to the Z-test.
</ConceptBox>

## A/B Testing

A/B testing is a two-sample test on a randomized experiment: group A (control, current version) vs group B (treatment). For a **binary** outcome (click / no click), use the **two-proportion Z-test**. With $\hat{p}_A = x_A/n_A$, $\hat{p}_B = x_B/n_B$:

$$H_0 : p_A = p_B \quad \text{vs} \quad H_1 : p_A \neq p_B$$

Under $H_0$ both share a common proportion, estimated by pooling:

$$\hat{p} = \frac{n_A \hat{p}_A + n_B \hat{p}_B}{n_A + n_B}, \qquad Z = \frac{\hat{p}_A - \hat{p}_B}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})\left(\frac{1}{n_A} + \frac{1}{n_B}\right)}}$$

Under $H_0$, $Z \sim N(0,1)$ when the normal approximation holds ($n\hat{p}$ and $n(1-\hat{p}) \geq 5$ for both groups).

<ConceptBox title="Statistical vs. practical significance">
Practitioners frequently violate this: with a large enough sample, *any* difference, however negligible, produces a tiny p-value. Always report an **effect size** (Cohen's d, or the raw difference in proportions) and the **confidence interval for the difference** — the CI makes the uncertainty visible where a bare reject/fail decision hides it.
</ConceptBox>

## Effect Size: Cohen's d

Significance says a difference is unlikely to be zero; it doesn't say the difference is large enough to matter. Cohen's d standardizes the magnitude:

$$d = \frac{|\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2|}{s_p}, \qquad s_p = \sqrt{\frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2 + (n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2}}$$

| d | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ≈ 0.2 | Small — distributions overlap heavily | height gap between 15- and 16-year-olds |
| ≈ 0.5 | Medium — visible to a careful observer | a structured reading intervention |
| ≈ 0.8 | Large — obvious in raw data | grip strength: athletes vs sedentary |

Cohen stressed these are rough guidelines — what counts as practically important depends on the domain and the decision's stakes.
