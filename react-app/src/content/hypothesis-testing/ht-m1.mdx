## What Is a Hypothesis Test?

A formal procedure for deciding between two competing claims about a population from a sample. The logic is indirect: ask whether the observed data would be very unlikely if a baseline claim were true.

The two claims are the **null hypothesis** $H_0$ and the **alternative** $H_1$. For a parameter $\theta$ and reference value $\theta_0$:

$$H_0 : \theta = \theta_0 \quad \text{vs} \quad H_1 : \theta \neq \theta_0 \;\; (\text{or } > \theta_0 \text{ or } < \theta_0)$$

For the drug trial, the parameter is the true mean recovery $\mu$, reference $\mu_0 = 10$, and the claim is faster recovery:

$$H_0 : \mu = 10 \quad \text{vs} \quad H_1 : \mu < 10$$

**$H_0$** is always the claim of no effect / status quo — assumed true by default. You never *prove* $H_0$; you only **reject** or **fail to reject** it. Failing to reject is not proof — just absence of strong enough evidence against it.

**$H_1$** is the research claim, stated in opposition to $H_0$, in one of three forms (chosen *before* seeing data — matching it to the observed pattern afterward invalidates the test):

$$\text{Two-tailed: } H_1 : \theta \neq \theta_0 \qquad \text{Left: } H_1 : \theta < \theta_0 \qquad \text{Right: } H_1 : \theta > \theta_0$$

<ConceptBox title="Innocent until proven guilty">
$H_0$ is the presumption of innocence. The burden of proof is on the data. You reject $H_0$ only when evidence clears a pre-specified bar; falling short gives "not guilty," never "definitely innocent." Absence of evidence against $H_0$ is not evidence for it.
</ConceptBox>

## Writing Hypotheses

The most consequential step — errors here can't be fixed by careful computation later.

<DerivationSteps>
<DerivationStep number={1} title="Identify the population parameter">
Usually $\mu$, $p$, or $\sigma^2$. It describes the *population*, never the sample.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={2} title="State H₀ as an equality">
$H_0 : \mu = \mu_0$ — a precise equality, because the test statistic is computed under it.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={3} title="State H₁ from the research question">
"different/changed" → two-tailed ($\neq$); "greater/increased" → right ($>$); "less/decreased/reduced" → left ($<$).
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={4} title="Never put a sample statistic in a hypothesis">
$H_0 : \bar{x} = 10$ is wrong — $\bar{x}$ is computed from data. Always $H_0 : \mu = 10$.
</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

## One-Tailed vs Two-Tailed

The direction of $H_1$ decides how the allowable error is split between the tails, which sets how strong the evidence must be.

- **Two-tailed** ($\neq$) — reject if the result is extreme in *either* direction; rejection region split across both tails. The scientific default; needs a stronger departure to reject at the same $\alpha$.
- **One-tailed** ($>$ or $<$) — reject only for extremes in one direction; whole rejection region in one tail. More powerful in that direction but blind to the opposite, so inappropriate for exploratory work.

**Guidance:** two-tailed is the conservative default. Only go one-tailed with a compelling pre-data argument that an opposite-direction effect would be irrelevant.

## The Six-Step Framework

Every test — Z, t, F, chi-squared — is this same logic:

<DerivationSteps>
<DerivationStep number={1} title="State H₀ and H₁">
In terms of the population parameter, explicit about direction.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={2} title="Choose the significance level α">
Max probability of wrongly rejecting a true $H_0$ you'll tolerate — commonly 0.05 or 0.01. Fixed *before* data; changing it after is manipulation. (Module 2.)
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={3} title="Compute the test statistic">
A single number summarizing how far the sample sits from what $H_0$ predicts, on a known distribution.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={4} title="Find the p-value or critical value">
p-value = probability of a statistic at least this extreme if $H_0$ were true. Critical value = the rejection threshold. Equivalent methods. (Module 2.)
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={5} title="Decide: reject or fail to reject">
p-value $< \alpha$ → reject. Otherwise fail to reject. Never "accept."
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={6} title="Conclude in plain language, in context">
Tie back to the research question — significance level, direction, and what it means.
</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

### The Test Statistic

It converts sample evidence into a position on a known distribution by standardizing — how many standard errors the estimate lies from the null value:

$$T = \frac{\hat{\theta} - \theta_0}{\text{SE}(\hat{\theta})}$$

$\hat{\theta}$ = sample estimate (e.g. $\bar{x}$), $\theta_0$ = null value, $\text{SE}$ = standard error. Under $H_0$ this follows a known distribution centered at zero: near zero = consistent with $H_0$; far in a tail = rare under $H_0$, i.e. evidence against it. Different tests use different reference distributions (Z, t, F, chi-squared), developed in Modules 3–5.

## Worked Example: The Drug Trial

**Step 1 — Hypotheses.** Parameter $\mu$ = true mean recovery. Status quo $\mu = 10$; the claim is a *decrease*:

$$H_0 : \mu = 10 \qquad H_1 : \mu < 10$$

A **left-tailed** test — reject only if the sample mean is substantially below 10.

**Step 2 — $\alpha$.** Set $\alpha = 0.05$: a 5% chance of concluding the drug works when it doesn't.

**Step 3 — Test statistic (preview, full computation in Module 3).** Suppose the data give

$$Z = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}} = -2.31$$

The sample mean sits 2.31 standard errors left of 10 — far into the left tail of the standard normal.

**Steps 4–5 — Decision.** Left-tailed critical value at $\alpha=0.05$ is $z^* = -1.645$. Since $-2.31 < -1.645$, the statistic is in the rejection region → **reject $H_0$**.

**Step 6 — Conclusion.** At the 5% level, there's sufficient evidence that the drug reduces mean recovery below 10 days; chance alone is an implausible explanation.

*This is statistical significance only. Whether the reduction is clinically meaningful, and whether the design supports causation, are separate questions.*

## Practice

A university claims graduates earn a mean starting salary of \$65,000. A student group thinks the true mean is *different* — higher or lower. They'll survey 45 graduates. Write the hypotheses and the test type.

**Solution:** Parameter $\mu$ = true mean starting salary.

$$H_0 : \mu = 65{,}000 \qquad H_1 : \mu \neq 65{,}000$$

**Two-tailed** — "different" signals no prior direction; both departures are equally interesting. ("Earn more than" would justify right-tailed.) Don't write $H_0 : \bar{x} = 65{,}000$ — $\bar{x}$ is a sample statistic; hypotheses describe the population.
