## Why This Module Exists

Autoregressive models (next module) assume the relationship between $y_t$ and its past values is the **same at every point in time**. If the series is trending or its variance is changing, that relationship keeps shifting under the model's feet — coefficients fit on one stretch stop applying to the next. Stationarity is the precondition that makes AR modelling well-posed.

## What "Stationary" Actually Means

<ConceptBox title="(Weak/covariance) stationarity — 3 conditions">
1. **Constant mean** — $E[y_t]$ doesn't depend on $t$.
2. **Constant variance** — $\text{Var}(y_t)$ doesn't depend on $t$ (no widening/narrowing spread).
3. **Autocovariance depends only on the lag, not on time** — $\text{Cov}(y_t, y_{t+k})$ is a function of $k$ alone, for every $t$.
</ConceptBox>

A trending series fails (1). A series whose swings grow over time fails (2). A series whose seasonal correlation shape changes decade to decade fails (3).

</PredictReveal>

]} />

## Testing for It: The ADF Test

<PredictReveal
  prompt="The augmented Dickey-Fuller test returns a large p-value on your series. What does that mean?"
  options={["The series is stationary, since large p-values are good", "You have failed to reject non-stationarity — its null hypothesis is that a unit root is present"]}
  correct={1}
>

The **Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF)** test is the standard formal check. It fits

$$\Delta y_t = \phi\, y_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\theta_i \Delta y_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t$$

and tests $H_0: \phi = 0$ (a **unit root** — non-stationary) against $H_1: \phi < 0$ (stationary). Small p-value (< 0.05) → reject $H_0$ → treat as stationary.

<ConceptBox title="Common confusion — ADF's null is 'non-stationary'">
This trips people up constantly: a **low** p-value is the *good* outcome here (stationary), the opposite of most tests. There's also a companion test, **KPSS**, whose null is the *reverse* ("series IS stationary") — running both and checking they agree is safer than trusting either alone.
</ConceptBox>

```python
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import adfuller

result = adfuller(y)
print(f'ADF statistic: {result[0]:.3f}, p-value: {result[1]:.4f}')
# p < 0.05  ->  reject H0  ->  series looks stationary
```

</PredictReveal>

<Recall items={[
  { q: "What is the null hypothesis of the augmented Dickey-Fuller test, and how do you read its p-value?", a: "The null is that the series has a unit root, that is, that it is non-stationary. A small p-value therefore rejects non-stationarity and supports stationarity, which is the opposite reading from most tests and a common source of error." },
]} />

## Dealing With Non-Stationarity

<PredictReveal
  prompt="Your series has a clear upward trend. You subtract each value from the next one. What have you done to the trend?"
  options={["Nothing — the differences still rise", "Removed it, because a constant slope becomes a constant difference"]}
  correct={1}
>

<DerivationSteps>
<DerivationStep number={1} title="Differencing — remove trend">
$$y_t' = y_t - y_{t-1}$$
Turns "the level trends" into "the change is roughly flat." Usually $d=1$ is enough; rarely $d=2$. Overdifferencing adds noise, so stop as soon as ADF says stationary.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={2} title="Seasonal differencing — remove a repeating cycle">
$$y_t' = y_t - y_{t-m}$$
Subtract the value from one full period ($m$) ago instead of one step ago — clears a seasonal pattern the way plain differencing clears a trend.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={3} title="Log / Box-Cox transform — remove growing variance">
$$y_t' = \log(y_t)$$
Fixes variance that grows with the level (the multiplicative case from Module 1), often applied *before* differencing.
</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

</PredictReveal>

<Recall items={[
  { q: "Why does differencing remove a trend?", a: "A linear trend adds the same amount at every step, so the difference between consecutive observations is constant and the drifting mean disappears. Differencing once removes a linear trend, twice removes a quadratic one, and seasonal differencing at the period removes a repeating seasonal pattern." },
]} />

## Autocorrelation: Measuring "How Long Is the Memory"

The **ACF** (autocorrelation function) is the correlation of the series with a lagged copy of itself:

$$\rho_k = \frac{\sum_t (y_t-\bar y)(y_{t-k}-\bar y)}{\sum_t (y_t-\bar y)^2}$$

A slowly-decaying ACF (still large at lag 15, 20…) is a visual tell for non-stationarity — the series "remembers" too far back, usually because of a trend. A properly stationary series' ACF drops toward the significance band ($\pm 1.96/\sqrt n$) within a handful of lags.

<ConceptBox title="TL;DR">
- Stationary = constant mean, constant variance, and autocovariance that depends only on the lag. AR models need it or their coefficients drift out of date.
- **ADF's null hypothesis is "non-stationary"** — so here a *low* p-value is the good news. Backwards from most tests.
- Three repair tools, one per symptom: difference for trend, seasonally difference for a repeating cycle, log for growing variance.
- A slowly-decaying ACF is the visual tell for non-stationarity.
</ConceptBox>

## Practice

**Problem 1.** ADF on a series gives p = 0.62. What do you conclude, and what do you try next?

**Solution:** Fail to reject $H_0$ → series looks non-stationary. Try first-differencing, then re-run ADF on the differenced series.

**Problem 2.** After first-differencing, the ACF still decays slowly at seasonal lags (12, 24, …). What's the fix?

**Solution:** Seasonal differencing ($y_t - y_{t-12}$ for monthly data) on top of the regular difference.
