<ConceptBox title="TL;DR">
- AR(p) is linear regression of the series **on its own past values**. MA(q) is a weighted sum of the last $q$ **shocks**. Both need the stationarity from Module 4, since the coefficients are assumed fixed over time.
- **Read $p$ off the PACF's cutoff and $q$ off the ACF's cutoff.** The cutoff swaps panels depending on which family you're in — that asymmetry is the whole identification rule.
- AR memory decays forever; MA memory stops dead after $q$ lags. That difference is *why* the cutoffs land where they do.
- **A prediction interval is not a confidence interval.** The PI includes one future point's own noise, so it is wider and it grows with the horizon.
</ConceptBox>

## The Autoregressive Model

**AR(p)** predicts $y_t$ as a linear combination of its own last $p$ values — "regress the series on itself":

$$y_t = c + \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \phi_2 y_{t-2} + \cdots + \phi_p y_{t-p} + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$$

$p$ is the **order**: how many past steps matter. The $\phi_i$ are fit from data — Module 4's stationarity requirement applies directly here, since these coefficients are assumed constant over time.

<ConceptBox title="Picking p — the PACF">
The **partial autocorrelation function (PACF)** measures the correlation between $y_t$ and $y_{t-k}$ with the effect of the *intermediate* lags removed. An AR(p) process has a PACF that cuts off sharply after lag $p$ — that cutoff point is the standard way to read off $p$ from `plot_pacf(y)`. (Contrast with the plain ACF, which decays gradually for an AR process — that asymmetry is exactly how you tell "this looks AR" from "this looks MA" in practice.)
</ConceptBox>

## Fitting: The Yule-Walker Equations

Multiply the AR equation by $y_{t-k}$ and take expectations — the $\phi_i$ solve a small linear system built entirely from the sample ACF:

<DerivationSteps>
<DerivationStep number={1} title="Set up">
$$\rho_k = \phi_1\rho_{k-1} + \phi_2\rho_{k-2} + \cdots + \phi_p \rho_{k-p}, \quad k=1,\dots,p$$
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={2} title="Solve">
This is $p$ equations, $p$ unknowns — a Toeplitz linear system in $\rho_0,\dots,\rho_{p-1}$ on the left, $\rho_1,\dots,\rho_p$ on the right. Solving it (Levinson-Durbin recursion, or plain Gaussian elimination for small $p$) gives $\hat\phi_1,\dots,\hat\phi_p$ directly from the sample autocorrelations computed in Module 4.
</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

## The Other Half of the Family: MA(q)

AR looks backward at past *values*. **MA(q)** looks backward at past *shocks* — the random errors that hit the series:

$$y_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t + \theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1} + \theta_2\varepsilon_{t-2} + \cdots + \theta_q\varepsilon_{t-q}, \qquad \varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$$

In words: today's value is the average level plus today's surprise plus a fading echo of the last $q$ surprises. The name is unfortunate — this is *not* the moving-average smoother from Module 2. Here it's a moving window over **shocks**, not over observations.

<ConceptBox title="Common confusion — 'how can I regress on errors I never observe?'">
You don't observe the $\varepsilon_t$, which is why MA models can't be fit by ordinary least squares the way AR models can. Fitting is iterative: guess $\theta$, run the series through to *infer* the implied residuals, score the likelihood, repeat. `statsmodels` does this for you with maximum likelihood — but it's the reason MA fits are slower and occasionally fail to converge, while a Yule-Walker AR fit is a one-shot linear solve.
</ConceptBox>

<ConceptBox title="Finite memory vs. infinite memory — the key contrast">
A shock at time $t$ affects an MA(q) series at $t, t+1, \dots, t+q$ and then **exactly nothing** afterwards. In an AR(p), that same shock feeds into $y_{t+1}$, which feeds into $y_{t+2}$, and so on forever — decaying geometrically but never reaching zero.

This is the mechanism behind the identification rule below. Two observations more than $q$ apart in an MA(q) share *no shocks at all*, so their correlation is genuinely zero → **the ACF cuts off at lag q**. In an AR process, every pair shares some history, so the ACF only ever tapers.
</ConceptBox>

<MAProcessWidget />

**Invertibility** is MA's counterpart to stationarity: if $|\theta| < 1$ (for MA(1)), the process can be rewritten as an infinite AR, which is what lets a finite history pin down the unobserved shocks. Non-invertible parameters give a model that fits equally well but can't be estimated uniquely — `statsmodels` will quietly return the invertible twin.

## Reading ACF and PACF Together

This is the **Box–Jenkins identification step**, and it's the single most useful table in classical forecasting:

| Pattern | ACF | PACF | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| **AR(p)** | decays (geometric or damped-sine) | **cuts off after lag p** | read $p$ off the PACF |
| **MA(q)** | **cuts off after lag q** | decays | read $q$ off the ACF |
| **ARMA(p,q)** | decays | decays | neither cuts off — pick the order by AIC |
| **Non-stationary** | decays *very* slowly, stays high | lag-1 spike near 1 | difference first (Module 4), then re-read |

<ConceptBox title="Common confusion — 'cuts off' vs 'decays'">
*Cuts off* means the bars fall inside the $\pm 1.96/\sqrt{n}$ band and **stay** there. *Decays* means they shrink gradually while remaining significant for several lags. On real data the distinction is rarely crisp — treat ACF/PACF as a shortlist generator (a few candidate orders), then let AIC pick the winner. Trusting the plots alone is how people end up over-fitting a spurious lag-7 spike.
</ConceptBox>

## Confidence Interval vs. Prediction Interval

This distinction matters more in forecasting than almost anywhere else in ML:

<ConceptBox title="Two different questions">
- **Confidence interval (CI)** — uncertainty about a **fixed quantity**, like the true mean or a coefficient. Reflects estimation error only. Roughly constant width.
- **Prediction interval (PI)** — uncertainty about **one future observed value** $y_{t+h}$. Reflects estimation error **plus** the irreducible noise $\varepsilon$ of that specific future point — and that noise compounds as $h$ grows, since each step's error feeds into the next. PIs are always wider than CIs, and they **widen with the forecast horizon**.
</ConceptBox>

For an AR(p), the $h$-step forecast variance is $\sigma^2 \sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j^2$, where $\psi_j$ are the model's impulse-response ("MA($\infty$)") weights — more steps out, more compounded uncertainty, wider band.

## In Python

```python
from statsmodels.tsa.ar_model import AutoReg

model = AutoReg(y, lags=2, old_names=False).fit()
print(model.params)                       # [const, phi_1, phi_2]

pred = model.get_prediction(start=len(y), end=len(y) + 11)   # 12-step forecast
forecast = pred.predicted_mean
ci = pred.conf_int(alpha=0.05)            # 95% prediction interval, widens with horizon
```

For an MA or a mixed model, use the general `ARIMA` interface — `order=(p, d, q)` with `d=0` is exactly ARMA:

```python
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from statsmodels.graphics.tsaplots import plot_acf, plot_pacf
from statsmodels.tsa.arima.model import ARIMA

fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 3))
plot_acf(y, lags=24, ax=ax[0])            # cutoff here -> q
plot_pacf(y, lags=24, method='ywm', ax=ax[1])   # cutoff here -> p

ma = ARIMA(y, order=(0, 0, 1)).fit()      # MA(1)
arma = ARIMA(y, order=(1, 0, 1)).fit()    # ARMA(1,1)
print(ma.aic, arma.aic)                   # lower AIC wins
```

Adding $d$ and the seasonal block to that same `order` argument is all that separates this from ARIMA and SARIMA — which is Module 6.

## Practice

**Problem 1.** PACF cuts off sharply after lag 2; ACF decays gradually. What order AR model, and why not check ACF's cutoff instead?

**Solution:** AR(2). Plain ACF for an AR process decays gradually regardless of $p$ — it can't tell you the order. PACF isolates each lag's *direct* contribution, so its cutoff point is the informative signal.

**Problem 2.** Two forecasts are quoted for $y_{t+1}$: a 95% CI of [48, 52] and a 95% PI of [40, 60]. Which is which, and why the gap?

**Solution:** [48, 52] is the CI (uncertainty in the estimated mean level only); [40, 60] is the PI (adds the actual noise variance of a single future draw) — the PI is always the wider one.

**Problem 3.** The ACF has one big spike at lag 1 and nothing significant after it; the PACF tapers off over five or six lags. Which model, and what does that say about how long a shock survives?

**Solution:** MA(1). A shock affects only today and tomorrow, then vanishes completely — which is exactly why observations two or more steps apart share no shocks and the ACF is flat beyond lag 1.
