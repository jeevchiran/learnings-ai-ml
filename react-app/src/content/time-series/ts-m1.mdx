## What Is a Time Series?

A **time series** is a sequence of values indexed in time order: daily stock prices, monthly sales, hourly server load. Two things make it different from a regular tabular dataset — **order matters** (shuffling rows destroys the signal), and **observations are usually not independent** (today's value leans on yesterday's).

| Domain | Series |
|---|---|
| Retail | Monthly revenue |
| Finance | Daily closing price |
| Ops / SRE | Requests-per-minute |
| Climate | Daily temperature |
| Epidemiology | Weekly case counts |

## The Components of a Time Series

Any series can be thought of as a mix of up to four pieces:

<ConceptBox title="Trend, Seasonality, Cycle, Residual">
- **Trend (T)** — the long-run direction: steadily up, down, or flat.
- **Seasonality (S)** — a pattern that repeats at a **fixed, known** period (every 12 months, every 7 days).
- **Cycle (C)** — rises and falls with **no fixed period** (business cycles, multi-year), often folded into "trend" in practice since it's hard to separate.
- **Residual / Noise (R)** — whatever is left after removing the above — ideally close to random.
</ConceptBox>

## Additive vs. Multiplicative

The two components combine in one of two ways:

$$\text{Additive: } y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t \qquad\qquad \text{Multiplicative: } y_t = T_t \times S_t \times R_t$$

**Additive** — seasonal swings stay a *constant size* no matter how high the trend climbs (e.g. "+200 units every December, always"). **Multiplicative** — seasonal swings grow *proportionally* with the trend (e.g. "+20% every December" — bigger in absolute terms as the base grows). A quick visual test: if the amplitude of the wiggles grows alongside the level, it's multiplicative; a log-transform turns a multiplicative series additive, since $\log(T\times S \times R) = \log T + \log S + \log R$.

## Decomposing in Python

`statsmodels` does the trend/seasonal/residual split for you — the widget above implements the same centered-moving-average idea by hand.

```python
import pandas as pd
from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import seasonal_decompose

# monthly series, 4 years, indexed by date
df = pd.read_csv('sales.csv', parse_dates=['date'], index_col='date')

result = seasonal_decompose(df['sales'], model='additive', period=12)
result.plot()   # 4 stacked panels: observed, trend, seasonal, residual

trend, seasonal, residual = result.trend, result.seasonal, result.resid
```

<ConceptBox title="Common confusion — trend vs. stationarity">
A series can have zero trend and still be non-stationary (e.g. its *variance* grows over time), and a trending series is by definition non-stationary. "Stationary" is a stronger, more precise condition than "no visible trend" — Module 4 makes this exact.

## Practice

**Problem 1.** A retailer's monthly sales show a rising baseline, and the December spike over the baseline grows larger every year in absolute units. Additive or multiplicative?

**Solution:** Multiplicative — the seasonal effect scales with the trend level.

**Problem 2.** You log-transform a multiplicative series and decompose it as additive. Why does this work?

**Solution:** $\log(T \cdot S \cdot R) = \log T + \log S + \log R$ — the product becomes a sum in log-space, so additive tools apply directly; exponentiate the result to get back to the original scale.
