# Module 14: Limitations of Region-Based Methods

<ConceptBox title="TL;DR">
- Faster R-CNN removed the redundant backbone and the hand-designed proposals. What survives is the **per-region head, still run 300 times**.
- That head is the reason two-stage detectors stall around **5–7 fps**: the cost is $O(\text{proposals})$, not $O(\text{image})$.
- The architecture is also **sequential** — propose, then classify — so latency is the sum of two stages and neither can start early.
- Two-stage detectors are still the accuracy leaders on **small and crowded objects**, because RoI pooling gives each candidate a dedicated, aligned feature.
- The one-stage answer: delete the proposal stage entirely and let a **single dense pass** emit class and box together. That is Modules 15–19.
</ConceptBox>

<Bridge>
Modules 12 and 13 were a story of deletions — redundant CNN passes, disjoint training, slow proposals. This module asks what is left, decides it is still too much, and states precisely what a single-shot detector has to give up to remove it.
</Bridge>

## What is left to delete

Faster R-CNN, VGG16, per image:

| Stage | Time | Scales with |
|---|---|---|
| Backbone conv | 146 ms | image area |
| RPN | 10 ms | image area |
| **RoI heads (300 regions)** | **42 ms** | **number of proposals** |

The backbone is irreducible — you must look at the image. The RPN is nearly free. The third row is the problem: it is the only term that grows with the number of candidates, and it is *the same dense head evaluated 300 times on overlapping crops of one feature map*. The exact structural complaint Module 12 made about R-CNN, one order of magnitude smaller.

## Four structural limitations

**1. Cost is proportional to proposals.** Want higher recall on a crowded scene? Raise the proposal count and latency rises with it. A one-stage detector's cost does not depend on how many objects are present.

**2. The pipeline is sequential.** The head cannot start before proposals exist, so latency is the sum, not the max. On fixed hardware this caps throughput even with perfect parallelism inside each stage.

**3. Many moving parts to tune.** Anchor scales and ratios, RPN positive/negative IoU cut-offs (0.7/0.3), pre- and post-NMS proposal counts, the RoI-head sampling ratio, two NMS thresholds. Each is a hyperparameter that has to be re-tuned when your data does not look like COCO.

**4. Duplicated work at the head.** Two proposals overlapping by 90% pool nearly identical features and run the same dense layers on both. The redundancy R-CNN had at the backbone still exists at the head.

<ConceptBox title="What two-stage detectors are still better at">
Do not read this module as "region-based lost". As of the modern literature, two-stage designs remain competitive or ahead on:

- **Small objects**, because RoI Align gives every candidate a dedicated feature grid at the resolution the object needs, rather than whatever one grid cell offers.
- **Crowded scenes**, because proposals can overlap freely, while a grid cell in YOLO v1 can only own one object.
- **High-IoU accuracy** (COCO AP@0.75+), because the second stage refines a box that has already been coarsely placed — two bites at localisation instead of one.

The trade is latency, and which side you want depends entirely on whether you are running on a workstation or on a battery.
</ConceptBox>

## What one-stage detectors give up

Deleting the proposal stage costs real things, and the next five modules are largely about paying those costs back:

| Given up | Consequence | Paid back by |
|---|---|---|
| Region-aligned features | one grid cell must describe whatever falls in it | multi-scale maps (SSD, FPN) |
| Two-step box refinement | one shot at localisation | anchors as good priors (Module 18) |
| Balanced RoI sampling | ~99.9% of dense predictions are background | hard negative mining, focal loss |
| Variable candidate count | a fixed grid caps objects per cell | more anchors per cell |

Row three deserves emphasis. Faster R-CNN's head sees a **sampled, roughly balanced** minibatch of RoIs. A one-stage detector computes a loss over *every* box on the grid — 8,732 for SSD300, over 100,000 for a modern FPN detector — and essentially all of them are background. The gradient from the easy negatives can drown the positives entirely. SSD handles it with hard negative mining at 3:1; RetinaNet's focal loss handles it by down-weighting easy examples continuously, and that paper's title — *Focal Loss for Dense Object Detection* — is precisely a statement that class imbalance was the thing standing between one-stage speed and two-stage accuracy.

## Where the field went next

- **FPN (2017)** — a top-down pathway that gives *every* scale strong semantics. Adopted by both families; it is why the two-stage/one-stage gap on small objects narrowed.
- **RetinaNet (2017)** — one-stage + focal loss, first to match two-stage accuracy at one-stage speed.
- **Anchor-free (FCOS, CenterNet, 2019)** — predict per-pixel distances to the box sides, or a centre heatmap; removes the anchor hyperparameters entirely.
- **DETR (2020)** — a transformer emitting a fixed set of 100 slots, matched to ground truth by the Hungarian algorithm. **No anchors, no NMS** — the duplicate problem is solved in the loss instead of in post-processing.

Every one of those is an attack on something named in this module.
