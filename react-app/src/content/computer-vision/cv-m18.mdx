## The problem anchors solve

A convolutional feature at one position produces a fixed-length vector. Ask it to emit "a box" and two things are ill-posed:

- **Which box?** If two objects overlap this position, which one is the target? Without a rule, the same output slot receives contradictory gradients on different images and learns the average of a tall box and a wide one — a useless square.
- **From what starting point?** Regressing $(w, h)$ from scratch means the network must learn the whole size distribution in its final layer, and early in training its outputs are noise.

Anchors answer both. Pre-assign $k$ shapes per position; match each ground truth to its best-overlapping anchor; predict a *correction*.

$$\text{prediction} = \text{anchor} + \text{learned offset}$$

The target is now near zero, well conditioned, and unambiguous.

## Choosing the shapes

<PredictReveal
  prompt="Anchor shapes are chosen by k-means on the training boxes — but not with ordinary Euclidean distance on (w, h). What distance function replaces it?"
  options={["Euclidean distance, just normalised first", "1 − IoU between the box and the centroid", "Manhattan distance on (w, h)"]}
  correct={1}
>

<AnchorBoxWidget />

Hand-picked anchors (Faster R-CNN used 3 scales × 3 ratios = 9) work, but the shapes in your data are not the shapes in COCO. YOLOv2 ran **k-means on the training set's box dimensions**, with a critical modification:

$$d(\text{box}, \text{centroid}) = 1 - \text{IoU}(\text{box}, \text{centroid})$$

<ConceptBox title="Why not Euclidean k-means">
Euclidean distance on $(w, h)$ makes the error from a 400×300 box roughly 100× the error from a 40×30 box of the same *relative* misfit. Clustering would spend all its centroids on large boxes and leave small ones sharing one bad anchor — exactly backwards, since small objects are the hard case.

$1 - \text{IoU}$ is scale-free: a 2× shape mismatch costs the same whether the boxes are 20 px or 200 px. The objective being clustered is then the thing you actually care about — how well the best anchor covers a typical ground truth.
</ConceptBox>

The elbow in the mean-IoU curve is the real answer to "how many anchors". YOLOv2 chose **5** as the accuracy/cost knee; v3 uses **9**, split three per scale across three feature maps.

</PredictReveal>

<Recall items={[

## Assignment during training

<PredictReveal
  prompt="An anchor has IoU 0.5 with the nearest ground truth — above the negative threshold but below the positive one. How does training treat it?"
  options={["As a positive — better to over-assign", "As a negative — safer to suppress", "It gets no gradient at all — ignored"]}
  correct={2}
>

For every anchor at every position:

| Condition | Label |
|---|---|
| Highest IoU with some ground truth (even if low) | **positive** — guarantees no object is unassigned |
| IoU > 0.7 (RPN) / > 0.5 (SSD) with any ground truth | **positive** |
| IoU < 0.3 (RPN) / < 0.4 (SSD) with all ground truths | **negative** |
| In between | **ignored** — contributes no gradient at all |

The ignore band matters. An anchor at IoU 0.5 is neither clearly right nor clearly wrong; training it as a negative teaches the network to suppress a nearly-correct box, and as a positive it teaches sloppiness. Excluding it is cleaner than guessing.

</PredictReveal>

<Recall items={[
  { q: "What is the 'ignore band' in anchor assignment for?", a: "It excludes anchors whose IoU with a ground truth is neither clearly high nor clearly low — say between 0.3 and 0.7 for the RPN. Calling such an anchor negative would train the network to suppress a nearly-correct box; calling it positive would reward sloppy localisation. Giving it no gradient at all is cleaner than guessing which." },
]} />

## The parameterisation, and the bug it fixed

<PredictReveal
  prompt="Faster R-CNN's raw offset t_x is unbounded — nothing stops a random init from predicting t_x = 30 and throwing the box across the image. What did YOLOv2 change to fix the resulting training instability?"
  options={["Clipped t_x to a fixed numeric range", "Wrapped the centre offset in a sigmoid, tying it to its own cell", "Dropped the centre offset and predicted absolute pixel coordinates"]}
  correct={1}
>

Faster R-CNN's offsets are unbounded:

$$x = x_a + t_x w_a, \qquad w = w_a e^{t_w}$$

$t_x = 1$ shifts the box a full anchor-width right. Nothing stops a randomly-initialised network from predicting $t_x = 30$ and putting the box on the other side of the image — and YOLOv2's authors report exactly that: the model was unstable for the first iterations, with most of the instability coming from the unconstrained centre coordinates.

Their fix ties the centre to the cell it was predicted in:

$$b_x = \sigma(t_x) + c_x, \qquad b_y = \sigma(t_y) + c_y, \qquad b_w = p_w e^{t_w}, \qquad b_h = p_h e^{t_h}$$

$\sigma$ bounds the centre offset to $[0,1)$, so **a prediction can never leave its own cell**, while $\exp$ keeps sizes positive and symmetric under doubling/halving (Module 13's derivation). Size stays unbounded because an object legitimately can be much larger than its anchor; position does not, because the responsible cell is a fact.

</PredictReveal>

<Recall items={[
  { q: "Why does YOLOv2 put a sigmoid on the centre offsets but an exponential on the sizes?", a: "Because the constraints differ. The responsible cell is a fact, so a centre offset should never leave it — the sigmoid bounds it to [0,1) and removes the early-training instability where an unconstrained offset threw boxes across the image. Size has no such bound: an object can legitimately be several times its anchor, so the exponential keeps it positive and symmetric under doubling and halving without capping it." },
]} />

## Worked example — decode one prediction

Cell $(c_x, c_y) = (4, 6)$ on a $13\times13$ grid, anchor $(p_w, p_h) = (1.87, 3.34)$ in **grid units**. Raw outputs $t_x = 0.4$, $t_y = -1.2$, $t_w = 0.25$, $t_h = -0.1$:

$$b_x = \sigma(0.4) + 4 = 0.599 + 4 = 4.599$$
$$b_y = \sigma(-1.2) + 6 = 0.231 + 6 = 6.231$$
$$b_w = 1.87\,e^{0.25} = 1.87 \times 1.284 = 2.401$$
$$b_h = 3.34\,e^{-0.1} = 3.34 \times 0.905 = 3.022$$

Divide by 13 for image fractions: centre $(0.354,\, 0.479)$, size $(0.185,\, 0.232)$. Note $t_y = -1.2$ did **not** move the box up out of the cell — the sigmoid clamped it to an offset of 0.231. That is the constraint doing its job.

## Anchor-free, and why the idea keeps returning

<PredictReveal
  prompt="Given everything above, do current state-of-the-art detectors like YOLOv8 still rely on anchor boxes?"
  options={["Yes — anchors remain standard", "No — modern detectors are anchor-free"]}
  correct={1}
>

Anchors are hyperparameters: how many, what shapes, which IoU cut-offs, per dataset. FCOS and CenterNet remove them:

| Method | Predicts | Advantage |
|---|---|---|
| **FCOS** | per pixel, the 4 distances to the box sides + a centreness score | no anchor tuning, no IoU thresholds |
| **CenterNet** | a heatmap peak per object + size at the peak | **no NMS** — peaks are already sparse |
| **YOLOv8** | anchor-free, with task-aligned assignment | fewer knobs, same accuracy |

Modern YOLO is anchor-free, so do not read Module 15–18 as describing current code. Read them as describing the *problem* anchors solved — target assignment and conditioning — because anchor-free methods still have to solve it, they just do it with a different rule.

</PredictReveal>

---

<DerivationSteps>
<DerivationStep number={1} title="Why 1 − IoU is the right k-means distance">

Standard k-means minimises $\sum_i \|x_i - \mu_{c(i)}\|^2$. On box dimensions that objective is **scale-dependent**: for boxes $(20, 30)$ and $(200, 300)$, a 10% shape error contributes $\approx 13$ and $\approx 1300$ respectively. The centroids chase the large boxes.

What you actually want to maximise is how well the assigned anchor covers a typical ground truth — mean IoU. So define

$$d(b, \mu) = 1 - \text{IoU}(b, \mu) = 1 - \frac{\min(w_b, w_\mu)\min(h_b, h_\mu)}{w_b h_b + w_\mu h_\mu - \min(w_b, w_\mu)\min(h_b, h_\mu)}$$

evaluating IoU with both boxes centred at the origin, since only *shape* is being clustered. This is invariant under scaling both boxes by the same factor, so a 2× mismatch costs the same at every size.

The algorithm is otherwise unchanged: assign each box to its nearest centroid under $d$, then recompute centroids as the mean of their members. Convergence is not guaranteed the way it is for Euclidean k-means (the mean does not minimise $d$ exactly), but in practice it settles in a few dozen iterations.

</DerivationStep>

<DerivationStep number={2} title="Counting anchors, and the imbalance they create">

YOLOv3 at $416\times416$ predicts on three feature maps with strides 32, 16 and 8:

$$13^2 + 26^2 + 52^2 = 169 + 676 + 2704 = 3{,}549 \text{ positions}$$

At 3 anchors per position: $3{,}549 \times 3 = \mathbf{10{,}647}$ boxes per image.

A typical COCO image has ~7 objects. Even generously counting 3 positive anchors each, that is ~21 positives against ~10,626 negatives — a ratio of about **1 : 500**. Summed naively, the background gradient dominates by two orders of magnitude and the model converges to "predict nothing", which scores well on the loss and 0 on mAP.

Three fixes, in historical order: YOLO's $\lambda_{\text{noobj}} = 0.5$ (a blunt constant), SSD's hard negative mining at 3:1 (Module 19), and RetinaNet's focal loss

$$FL(p_t) = -(1 - p_t)^\gamma \log p_t$$

which at $\gamma = 2$ scales an easy negative's contribution ($p_t = 0.99$) down by $10^{-4}$ while leaving a hard example ($p_t = 0.5$) at a quarter weight. **More anchors always means more imbalance** — the two problems are inseparable.

</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

<Recall items={[
  { q: "YOLOv3 at 416px produces 10,647 boxes for an image with about 7 objects. YOLO damps it with lambda_noobj = 0.5, SSD uses hard negative mining capped at 3 negatives per positive, and RetinaNet's focal loss down-weights easy examples continuously by (1-p)^gamma." },

---

<ConceptBox title="TL;DR">
- An anchor is a **fixed reference box** attached to a feature-map position. The network predicts a small correction to it, not a box from nothing.
- Anchors make target assignment well-posed: each ground truth is matched to the anchor it overlaps most, so every output slot has an unambiguous job.
- They also lift the one-object-per-cell ceiling — $k$ anchors per cell means $k$ objects of different shapes can coexist there.
- Shapes are chosen by **k-means on the training boxes with $d = 1 - \text{IoU}$**, not Euclidean distance, so large boxes do not dominate.
- YOLOv2's constrained parameterisation ($\sigma$ on the centre offsets, $\exp$ on the sizes) is what made anchors trainable from a random init.
</ConceptBox>
