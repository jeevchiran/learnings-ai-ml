An image is numbers in a grid. Nothing more. Every idea below is a consequence of that one fact.

## The array

<PredictReveal
  prompt="A greyscale image loads with shape (480, 640). Which number is the width?"
  options={["480", "640", "Can't tell without more info"]}
  correct={1}
>

Shape is `(H, W)` for greyscale, `(H, W, 3)` for colour — height first. NumPy indexes the same way: `img[y, x]`, row before column. That is the opposite of the `(x, y)` order drawing functions like `cv2.rectangle` take, and it is the single most common source of a swapped-box bug.

<PixelInspectorWidget />

Formally, a greyscale image is a function sampled on a grid:

$$I : \{0,\dots,H-1\} \times \{0,\dots,W-1\} \rightarrow \{0,\dots,255\}$$

## Sampling and quantisation

<PredictReveal
  prompt="You take a smooth gradient photo and drop it from 8-bit to 4-bit. What artefact shows up?"
  options={["Jagged, staircase edges", "Visible banding in the gradient", "Nothing — 4-bit is still smooth"]}
  correct={1}
>

Two independent, both-lossy discretisations produced every pixel: **sampling** (how many pixels — controlled by sensor resolution, too coarse gives jagged edges/aliasing) and **quantisation** (how many values each pixel may hold — controlled by bit depth, too coarse gives banding). Your guess was about quantisation: fewer intensity levels means a smooth gradient becomes visible steps. The pixel positions are untouched — that's sampling's job, not quantisation's.

A 1920×1080 RGB frame at 8 bits is $1920 \times 1080 \times 3 = 6{,}220{,}800$ bytes — just under 6 MB, uncompressed, for one frame.

</PredictReveal>

## Greyscale is not the average

<PredictReveal
  prompt="You convert a pure-green patch and a pure-blue patch to greyscale by averaging (R+G+B)/3. Do they come out equally bright?"
  options={["Yes — averaging is colour-blind by design", "No — green comes out brighter"]}
  correct={1}
>

The eye is most sensitive to green, so the standard luma weights are not an even split:

$$0.299R + 0.587G + 0.114B$$

Average the channels instead and a pure-green patch and a pure-blue patch land at the same "brightness" even though one looks far brighter to you. That mismatch is why greyscale conversion uses fixed, uneven weights rather than a mean.

</PredictReveal>

## Colour is three arrays stacked

<PredictReveal
  prompt="You're thresholding a red mug under changing room light. Better to threshold in RGB or HSV?"
  options={["RGB", "HSV"]}
  correct={1}
>

An RGB image is $(H, W, 3)$: three greyscale planes read together — a storage convention, not a law of nature. **HSV** separates colour identity (hue) from brightness (value). Under changing light, an object's RGB triple moves along all three axes at once; its hue barely moves. A hue *range* is a stable rule where an RGB box keeps sliding out from under you — which is why colour thresholding works in HSV and fights you in RGB.

## Two conventions that bite

<PredictReveal
  prompt="You load a photo with cv2.imread and hand it straight to plt.imshow, no conversion. What do you see?"
  options={["The photo, correctly", "Red and blue swapped"]}
  correct={1}
>

**BGR, not RGB.** OpenCV's `imread` returns channels in **B, G, R** order for historical reasons; Matplotlib expects RGB. Displaying an OpenCV image directly swaps red and blue — the classic "why is the sky orange" bug. Fix: `img[:, :, ::-1]` or `cv2.cvtColor(img, cv2.COLOR_BGR2RGB)`.

The row-major indexing from the first section has the same shape of bug: `img[y, x]` in NumPy, `cv2.rectangle(img, (x, y), ...)` in OpenCV's own drawing calls — same library, opposite order.

</PredictReveal>

## Worked example — memory and dtype

A 4000×3000 photo, 3 channels:

$$4000 \times 3000 \times 3 \times 1\text{ byte} = 36{,}000{,}000 \text{ bytes} \approx 34.3 \text{ MiB}$$

Convert it to `float32` for a model and it becomes $4\times$ that — **137 MiB per image**. A batch of 32 is 4.4 GiB before a single layer runs. This is why data pipelines resize *before* converting to float, not after.

```python
import cv2, numpy as np

img = cv2.imread('photo.jpg')          # (3000, 4000, 3), dtype=uint8, BGR
print(img.shape, img.dtype, img.nbytes / 1e6, 'MB')

grey = cv2.cvtColor(img, cv2.COLOR_BGR2GRAY)   # (3000, 4000) — channel axis gone
hsv  = cv2.cvtColor(img, cv2.COLOR_BGR2HSV)    # H in [0,179] for uint8, not [0,359]

print(img[100, 200])        # [B G R] at row 100, column 200
print(grey.min(), grey.max(), grey.mean().round(1))
```

Note the OpenCV quirk in that last comment: hue is halved to fit 0–255 in a `uint8`, so "red" is near 0 **or** near 179, not 359.

<ConceptBox title="TL;DR">
- An image is a **2-D (or 3-D) array of integers**. Everything above is arithmetic on that array.
- Shape is `(H, W)` greyscale or `(H, W, 3)` colour. NumPy indexes `img[y, x]` — **row first**, the opposite of the `(x, y)` order drawing functions take.
- **Sampling** decides how many pixels; **quantisation** decides how many values each pixel may hold. Both are lossy, both happen before your model sees anything.
- Greyscale is not the channel average: $0.299R + 0.587G + 0.114B$, because the eye weights green most.
- **HSV** separates colour from brightness; RGB does not. That is why colour thresholding works in HSV and fails in RGB.
</ConceptBox>
