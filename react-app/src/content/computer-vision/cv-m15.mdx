# Module 15: YOLO — Introduction and How It Works

<ConceptBox title="TL;DR">
- **You Only Look Once**: detection as a single regression from image pixels to a grid of boxes and class probabilities. No proposals, no second stage.
- The image is divided into an $S \times S$ grid. The cell containing an object's **centre** is responsible for detecting it.
- Output is one tensor: $S \times S \times (B \cdot 5 + C)$. For v1, $7 \times 7 \times 30 = 1{,}470$ numbers.
- Each box carries $(x, y, w, h, \text{confidence})$ where confidence is trained toward $\Pr(\text{object}) \times \text{IoU}$ — it predicts its own correctness.
- v1 was **45 fps at 63.4 mAP** — faster than anything, less accurate than Faster R-CNN, and structurally unable to detect more than one class per cell.
</ConceptBox>

<Bridge>
Module 14 identified the per-region head as the last redundancy in two-stage detection. YOLO's answer is not to make the head cheaper but to delete the concept of a region entirely: one forward pass produces every box for the whole image at once.
</Bridge>

## The reframing

Faster R-CNN asks "which regions might contain objects, and what is in each?" YOLO asks a single question of the whole image and answers it in one tensor. Three consequences fall straight out:

- **It sees global context.** The whole image is in every prediction's receptive field, so YOLO makes roughly half as many background false positives as Fast R-CNN — a patch of texture that looks like a dog in isolation does not, in context.
- **It generalises across domains.** Trained on natural images and tested on artwork, YOLO degrades far less than R-CNN, because it is not relying on a proposal algorithm tuned to photographic statistics.
- **Localisation is weaker.** One shot at the box, from a coarse grid, and no refinement stage. In the v1 error analysis, localisation is the dominant error type.

## The output tensor

<YOLOGridWidget />

Each cell predicts:

- $B$ boxes, each $(x, y, w, h, \text{conf})$ — 5 numbers,
- $C$ class probabilities, **shared across the cell's boxes**.

$$\text{output} = S \times S \times (B \cdot 5 + C)$$

YOLO v1: $S=7$, $B=2$, $C=20$ → $7 \times 7 \times 30 = 1{,}470$ numbers, and **98 boxes** total ($7 \times 7 \times 2$). Compare SSD300's 8,732 in Module 19.

**Coordinate conventions matter here:**

| Field | Range | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| $x, y$ | $[0, 1)$ | offset **inside the responsible cell** |
| $w, h$ | $[0, 1]$ | fraction of the **whole image** |
| conf | $[0, 1]$ | $\Pr(\text{object}) \times \text{IoU}^{\text{truth}}_{\text{pred}}$ |

Two different normalisations on purpose: $x,y$ relative to the cell keeps the target near the middle of the sigmoid's useful range and makes the prediction translation-equivariant; $w,h$ relative to the image because a box may be much larger than a cell.

The class-specific score used at test time multiplies the two:

$$\Pr(\text{class}_i \mid \text{object}) \times \Pr(\text{object}) \times \text{IoU} = \Pr(\text{class}_i) \times \text{IoU}$$

so a box that is confidently a dog but poorly localised scores low — the ranking that NMS then consumes already accounts for localisation quality.

## Responsibility, and the loss

Exactly one cell is responsible for each object: the one containing its centre. Within that cell, the one of $B$ predictors with the **highest current IoU** against the ground truth is responsible. This is a form of specialisation — over training, one predictor drifts toward tall boxes and the other toward wide ones.

$$
\begin{aligned}
L =\;& \lambda_{\text{coord}} \sum_{i=0}^{S^2}\sum_{j=0}^{B} \mathbb{1}^{\text{obj}}_{ij}\Big[(x_i - \hat x_i)^2 + (y_i - \hat y_i)^2\Big] \\
+\;& \lambda_{\text{coord}} \sum_{i=0}^{S^2}\sum_{j=0}^{B} \mathbb{1}^{\text{obj}}_{ij}\Big[(\sqrt{w_i} - \sqrt{\hat w_i})^2 + (\sqrt{h_i} - \sqrt{\hat h_i})^2\Big] \\
+\;& \sum_{i=0}^{S^2}\sum_{j=0}^{B} \mathbb{1}^{\text{obj}}_{ij}(C_i - \hat C_i)^2 \;+\; \lambda_{\text{noobj}} \sum_{i=0}^{S^2}\sum_{j=0}^{B} \mathbb{1}^{\text{noobj}}_{ij}(C_i - \hat C_i)^2 \\
+\;& \sum_{i=0}^{S^2} \mathbb{1}^{\text{obj}}_{i} \sum_{c \in \text{classes}} (p_i(c) - \hat p_i(c))^2
\end{aligned}
$$

with $\lambda_{\text{coord}} = 5$ and $\lambda_{\text{noobj}} = 0.5$. Three things in there are load-bearing:

- The **indicators** are Module 9's masking, applied per cell and per predictor.
- $\lambda_{\text{noobj}} = 0.5$ because most of the 49 cells are empty; without the damping, their confidence gradients overwhelm everything else. This is the class-imbalance problem from Module 14, handled crudely.
- The **square roots on $w$ and $h$**: see the derivation.

## Limitations, stated plainly

| Limitation | Cause | Fixed in |
|---|---|---|
| Max one class per cell | $C$ probabilities are per *cell*, not per box | v2 (per-box classes) |
| Struggles with flocks/crowds | 2 boxes per cell, one class | v2/v3 (more anchors) |
| Coarse localisation | $S = 7$, single scale, no refinement | v2 (anchors), v3 (3 scales) |
| Poor on unusual aspect ratios | boxes regressed from scratch | v2 (anchor priors, Module 18) |

Drag both objects into the same cell in the widget — one of them is simply unrepresentable. That is not a training failure, it is an architectural ceiling, and removing it is what the v2 rewrite is about.

<DerivationSteps>
<DerivationStep number={1} title="Why the loss uses √w and √h">

Squared error on raw width treats absolute deviations equally: 10 pixels of error costs the same on a 20-pixel box (catastrophic) and a 400-pixel box (irrelevant). But IoU, the thing you are judged on, is a *relative* measure.

Taking the square root compresses the large values and stretches the small ones. The derivative of the penalty with respect to a width error is

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial \hat w}(\sqrt{w} - \sqrt{\hat w})^2 = -\frac{\sqrt{w} - \sqrt{\hat w}}{\sqrt{\hat w}}$$

which grows as $\hat w \to 0$: small boxes get proportionally larger gradients for the same absolute error. Concretely, a 5-unit error on $w = 25$ costs $(\sqrt{25} - \sqrt{30})^2 = 0.228$, while the same 5-unit error on $w = 200$ costs $(\sqrt{200} - \sqrt{205})^2 = 0.031$ — about **7× less**. That is the intended asymmetry.

v2 onward replaces this with the $\log$ parameterisation from Module 13, which achieves the same scale-freeness more cleanly and is symmetric under doubling and halving.

</DerivationStep>

<DerivationStep number={2} title="What the confidence score is actually trained to be">

The target for a responsible predictor is not 1 — it is

$$\hat C = \Pr(\text{object}) \times \text{IoU}^{\text{truth}}_{\text{pred}}$$

computed against the ground truth **at that moment in training**. So the network is asked to predict *how good its own box is*, not merely whether something is there.

Why this matters: at test time the ranking fed to NMS is $\Pr(\text{class}) \times \text{IoU}$. A box that is certainly a dog but badly placed gets a low score and loses NMS to a better-placed one. Confidence and localisation quality are entangled deliberately, so the greedy suppression in Module 10 selects for both instead of for class certainty alone.

The cost is a moving target — the label depends on the current prediction — which makes early training unstable. v2's use of anchors gives the IoU a sensible starting value and largely removes the instability.

</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>
