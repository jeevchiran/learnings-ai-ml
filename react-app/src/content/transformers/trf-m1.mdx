# Module 1: Intuition Behind a Transformer

<Bridge>
The Attention & Encoder-Decoder track ended on a specific, narrow limitation: the decoder attends to the encoder, but everything inside the encoder and inside the decoder is still a strictly sequential RNN. This track asks the question that limitation was pointing at — what if attention was not a bolt-on fix for one bottleneck, but the entire mechanism? ## Every word starts as a guess, then gets refined

  prompt='The word "creature" on its own could mean almost anything — an animal, a monster, a robot. In the sentence above, what narrows it down?'
  options={["Nothing — the word creature always means the same thing", "The words around it: fluffy, blue, roamed, forest"]}
  correct={1}
>

This is the whole idea, and everything else in the track is machinery for doing it well.

Each **token** starts with a vector from a learned embedding table. A token may be a word or part of a word. Attention updates its representation by mixing information from allowed positions. Think of “which nearby words help interpret this token?” as an analogy: the model computes learned numerical scores, not literal grammatical questions. In a bidirectional encoder all non-padding positions may contribute; a causal decoder can use only the current and earlier positions.

"Creature" starts as a blank slate. After attention, it has absorbed "fluffy" and "blue": its vector now encodes *specifically* a fluffy blue creature, not creatures in general. Same word, same starting vector, different ending vector — because a different sentence surrounded it.

</PredictReveal>

## Self-attention vs the attention you already know

<PredictReveal
  prompt="In the previous track, the decoder attended to the encoder's hidden states — two different sequences. In a transformer, what does a word attend to?"
  options={["Only words in a separate sequence, like before", "Other words in its own sequence"]}
  correct={1}
>

That earlier mechanism is now called **cross-attention**: one sequence (the decoder) queries a different sequence (the encoder). A transformer adds a second kind, **self-attention**: every word in a sentence queries every *other* word in that same sentence. "Creature" is not looking outside itself for context — it is looking at its own neighbours, "fluffy" and "blue", to sharpen its own meaning.

Self-attention is what lets a transformer build a genuinely contextual representation of a whole sentence in one pass, rather than reading it one token at a time the way an RNN has to.

</PredictReveal>

<Recall items={[
  { q: "Distinguish self-attention from cross-attention.", a: "Self-attention is a sequence attending to itself — every word looks at every other word in the same sentence. Cross-attention is one sequence attending to a different one, like a decoder attending to an encoder's outputs. Transformers use both, but self-attention is the new mechanism this track focuses on." },
]} />

<ConceptBox title="Why this replaces recurrence">
An RNN builds context by reading left to right, one word updating a single hidden state at a time — which is exactly why it cannot be parallelised across time steps (Module 11 of the previous track). Self-attention lets every word look at every other word *simultaneously*, in one matrix multiplication. Context still gets built, but nothing has to wait its turn.
</ConceptBox>

## Where the starting vectors come from

The previous track's Module 1 already made this case: a static embedding table gives "bank" one vector whether the sentence is about a river or a savings account, because the vector is computed *before* the sentence is read. That ceiling is precisely what self-attention lifts.

Worth adding here is that "static embedding" is not one method but three families, and the third solves a problem the other two cannot:

<PredictReveal
  prompt="Word2Vec and GloVe both have a fixed vocabulary. Hand either of them a word that never appeared in training and you get nothing back. FastText represents a word as a bag of character n-grams instead. What does that buy?"
  options={["Faster lookup for common words", "A usable vector for a word it has never seen, assembled from familiar sub-word pieces"]}
  correct={1}
>

<EmbeddingMethodsWidget />

- **Word2Vec** learns by prediction. **CBOW** predicts a target word from its context; **skip-gram** predicts the context from the target and handles rare words better, because one occurrence produces a training pair per neighbour rather than a single averaged prediction.
- **GloVe** skips prediction and factorises a corpus-wide **co-occurrence count matrix**, so its vectors reflect global distributional statistics rather than many small local windows.
- **FastText** represents each word as a bag of **character n-grams**. Because those n-grams are shared across words, an unseen word can still be assembled from pieces the model knows — the one thing a fixed-vocabulary table cannot do at all.

That ceiling shows up as four separate failures, and it is worth naming all four because attention addresses each one:

<FactList accent="#1e3a8a" items={[
  { t: 'Context insensitivity', d: 'One vector per word, whatever the sentence. Bank by a river and bank with a savings account are the same numbers.' },
  { t: 'No grammatical role', d: 'The same vector whether the word is a subject, an object, a common noun or part of a name.' },
  { t: 'Short reach', d: 'Trained on a small local window, so a relationship between words ten positions apart is never observed.' },
  { t: 'No sentence-level meaning', d: 'Negation, emphasis and sarcasm live above the word. A bag of word vectors cannot represent them.' },
]} />

These methods introduce the idea of a context-independent lookup vector. A transformer normally learns its **own token embedding table jointly with the rest of the model**; it does not require Word2Vec, GloVe or FastText. The attention layers then create contextual representations from those initial vectors.

</PredictReveal>

<Recall items={[
  { q: "What can FastText do that Word2Vec and GloVe cannot, and why?", a: "Produce a vector for a word it never saw in training. FastText represents a word as the sum of its character n-gram vectors, and those n-grams are shared across words, so an unfamiliar word can be assembled from familiar pieces. Word2Vec and GloVe have fixed vocabularies with no entry at all for an out-of-vocabulary word." },
]} />

---

<ConceptBox title="TL;DR">
- A transformer's core move: every word **starts generic and gets refined** by looking at the other words in its sentence. The same word ends up with different vectors in different sentences.
- **Self-attention** is a sequence attending to itself; **cross-attention** (the previous track's mechanism) is one sequence attending to another. Transformers use both.
- Because every word can look at every other word at once, this runs **in parallel** — removing the sequential bottleneck an RNN has.
- Transformers normally learn their own token embedding table. Word2Vec, GloVe and FastText are useful earlier examples of embeddings, not required transformer components.
</ConceptBox>
