<Bridge>

## Where words come from: the static embedding families

  options={["CBOW — averaging context smooths out noise", "Skip-gram — each occurrence generates several training pairs instead of one"]}
  correct={1}
>

<EmbeddingMethodsWidget />

Before a transformer can refine anything, each word has to become a vector at all. Three families dominated this before transformers, and they are all still worth knowing because every one of them shows up inside a modern pipeline somewhere:

- **Word2Vec** learns embeddings by prediction. **CBOW** predicts a target word from its surrounding context words, which encourages the model to learn how groups of related words jointly indicate meaning. **Skip-gram** goes the other way, predicting surrounding context from a given target word — and because one occurrence of a rare word generates a separate training pair for each of its neighbours, skip-gram builds much stronger representations for infrequent words.
- **GloVe** skips prediction entirely and uses **global word co-occurrence statistics**, factorising a corpus-wide count matrix so the resulting vectors reflect overall distributional patterns rather than being inferred from many small local windows.
- **FastText** represents each word as a bag of **character n-grams**, letting it model internal word structure and — crucially — build a vector for a word it has never seen, by summing the n-gram vectors it does know.

</PredictReveal>

## Where static embeddings run out

<PredictReveal
  prompt='In a static embedding table, "bank" in "river bank" and "bank" in "savings bank" get which vectors?'
  options={["Two different vectors, chosen by context", "The exact same vector, both times"]}
  correct={1}
>

Every method above produces one fixed vector per word, learned once and looked up forever. That single design decision creates four distinct problems:

| Limitation | What breaks |
|---|---|
| **Context insensitivity** | A word gets the same representation even when its meaning changes — "bank" as a financial institution or a river edge is one vector holding an average of both senses. |
| **No grammatical or entity roles** | The same vector is used whether a word appears as a common noun, a named entity, or in a different syntactic position — "Apple" the company and "apple" the fruit collapse together. |
| **Weak long-range dependencies** | Training relies mainly on local context windows, so relationships spanning longer stretches of text are hard to capture. |
| **Poor sentence-level meaning** | Negation, emphasis, and sarcasm are properties of how words combine, not of any word alone — they are invisible at the word level. |

Every one of these traces back to the same root: the embedding is computed *before* the sentence is read. Fixing it means making a word's representation depend on the sentence it actually appears in — which is exactly what the rest of this track builds.

</PredictReveal>

All four trace back to the same root cause: the embedding is computed before the sentence is read, so it cannot depend on context." },
]} />

## Every word starts as a guess, then gets refined

<PredictReveal
  prompt='The word "creature" on its own could mean almost anything — an animal, a monster, a robot. In the sentence above, what narrows it down?'
  options={["Nothing — the word creature always means the same thing", "The words around it: fluffy, blue, roamed, forest"]}
  correct={1}
>

A transformer's core move is deceptively simple: every word starts with a generic, dictionary-style embedding — one of the static vectors above, which has to represent every possible sense of that word because it has not read the sentence yet. Then, one layer at a time, each word is allowed to look at every other word and ask a question like *"is there an adjective sitting in front of me?"* — and if the answer is yes, it pulls that meaning into itself.

"Creature" starts as a blank slate. After attention, it has absorbed "fluffy" and "blue" — its vector now encodes *specifically* a fluffy blue creature, not creatures in general.

</PredictReveal>

]} />

## Self-attention vs the attention you already know

<PredictReveal
  prompt="In the Attention & Encoder-Decoder track, the decoder attended to the encoder's hidden states — two different sequences. In a transformer, what does a word attend to?"
  options={["Only words in a separate sequence, like before", "Other words in its own sequence"]}
  correct={1}
>

That earlier mechanism is now called **cross-attention**: one sequence (the decoder) queries a different sequence (the encoder). A transformer adds a second kind, **self-attention**: every word in a sentence queries every *other* word in that same sentence. "Creature" is not looking outside itself for context — it is looking at its own neighbours, "fluffy" and "blue", to sharpen its own meaning.

Self-attention is what lets a transformer build a genuinely contextual representation of a whole sentence in one pass, rather than reading it one token at a time the way an RNN has to.

</PredictReveal>

<Recall items={[
  { q: "Distinguish self-attention from cross-attention.", a: "Self-attention is a sequence attending to itself — every word looks at every other word in the same sentence. Cross-attention is one sequence attending to a different one, like a decoder attending to an encoder's outputs. Transformers use both, but self-attention is the new mechanism this track focuses on." },
]} />

<ConceptBox title="Why this replaces recurrence">
An RNN builds context by reading left to right, one word updating a single hidden state at a time — which is exactly why it cannot be parallelised across time steps (Module 11 of the previous track). Self-attention lets every word look at every other word *simultaneously*, in one matrix multiplication. Context still gets built, but nothing has to wait its turn.
</ConceptBox>

---

<ConceptBox title="TL;DR">
- **Static embeddings** come in three families: Word2Vec (CBOW predicts target from context, skip-gram predicts context from target and handles rare words better), GloVe (factorises global co-occurrence counts), and FastText (sums character n-grams, so it can build vectors for unseen words).
- All of them share four limitations — context insensitivity, no grammatical or entity roles, weak long-range dependencies, and no sentence-level meaning — because the vector is computed before the sentence is read.
- A transformer refines every word's meaning by letting it look at the other words in the sentence and pull in relevant context, starting from exactly one of those static vectors.
- **Self-attention** is a sequence attending to itself; **cross-attention** is one sequence attending to another. Because every word can look at every other word at once, this runs in parallel — no sequential bottleneck like an RNN has.
</ConceptBox>
