## The feature hierarchy is the whole mechanism

A CNN trained on ImageNet does not learn "cat". It learns a **stack of increasingly specific detectors**, and the classifier on top is a thin layer that reads the last one:

| Depth | What it responds to | How ImageNet-specific |
|---|---|---|
| conv1 | oriented edges, colour blobs | almost none — this is Gabor filters, rediscovered |
| conv2 | corners, textures, simple patterns | very little |
| conv3 | motifs, repeated structure | some |
| conv4 | object parts — wheels, eyes, fur | substantial |
| conv5 | whole-object configurations | high |
| fc | the 1,000 ImageNet classes | total |

The first layer of essentially every trained vision model looks the same, whether it was trained on photographs, X-rays, or satellite tiles. That is not a coincidence — it is a statement about **natural image statistics**, which are dominated by edges at all scales regardless of subject.

## Domain distance beats dataset size

<PredictReveal
  prompt="On a domain very far from ImageNet — radar or seismic data — does starting from pretrained ImageNet weights typically help, hurt, or make no difference versus training from scratch?"
  options={["Always helps, just by less", "Can actually hurt — this is negative transfer", "No measurable difference either way"]}
  correct={1}
>

The instinct is that transfer learning is "for small datasets". It is more precisely for **datasets close to the source domain**, and the two get confused because most small datasets people try are ordinary photographs.

$$\text{usable depth} \approx f(\text{domain distance}), \quad \text{not } f(\text{n samples})$$

| Target | Distance | What actually happens |
|---|---|---|
| Pet breeds, food, retail products | very close | freeze almost everything; a linear head on frozen features often beats fine-tuning |
| Aerial / satellite | moderate | freeze the first half, fine-tune the rest |
| Chest X-ray, histology | far | freeze only the first block or two; expect to fine-tune most of the network |
| Radar, spectrograms, seismic | very far | transfer buys little; a smaller architecture trained from scratch may win |

<ConceptBox title="Negative transfer — the failure nobody warns you about">
On a sufficiently distant domain, initialising from ImageNet can be **worse than random initialisation**. The pretrained deep layers encode strong priors about object-like structure; on a domain where that prior is wrong, the optimiser has to *unlearn* it before it can learn anything, and it may settle in a worse basin than a fresh start would have.

The signal to watch for: fine-tuning converges quickly to a mediocre plateau and refuses to improve, while a from-scratch baseline is still climbing. Always run the from-scratch baseline. It is cheap and it is the only way to know transfer helped.
</ConceptBox>

</PredictReveal>

## The two things that quietly break transfer

<PredictReveal
  prompt="You set requires_grad = False on every parameter of a pretrained backbone that contains BatchNorm layers, then train. Does that fully freeze the backbone's behaviour?"
  options={["Yes — requires_grad=False freezes the whole module", "No — BatchNorm's running mean/variance keep updating unless you also call .eval()"]}
  correct={1}
>

**1. Normalisation mismatch.** The frozen weights were calibrated on inputs standardised with ImageNet's statistics. Feed them anything else and every activation is shifted:

```python
# The frozen backbone expects EXACTLY this, because that is what it saw in training
transforms.Normalize(mean=[0.485, 0.456, 0.406], std=[0.229, 0.224, 0.225])
```

Use your own dataset's statistics and a frozen backbone silently degrades. Use them with a *fully fine-tuned* backbone and it is fine, because the weights adapt. The rule: **frozen means you inherit the whole input contract**, normalisation included.

**2. BatchNorm in a "frozen" backbone.** Setting `requires_grad = False` freezes the *weights*. It does **not** stop BatchNorm from updating its running mean and variance, which happen in the forward pass and are not parameters:

```python
for p in model.parameters():
    p.requires_grad = False          # freezes weights — but NOT BN statistics

model.eval()                          # this is what actually freezes BN running stats
# or, per-module:
for m in model.modules():
    if isinstance(m, nn.BatchNorm2d):
        m.eval()
```

Miss this and your "frozen" feature extractor drifts across epochs, producing the baffling symptom of a frozen model whose validation accuracy changes.

</PredictReveal>

<Recall items={[

Why?", a: "BatchNorm's running mean and variance are buffers, not parameters, so they keep updating in the forward pass regardless of requires_grad. The fix is to put those modules in eval mode — model.eval() for the whole backbone, or setting each BatchNorm2d to eval individually — which is what actually stops the statistics from moving." },

A fully fine-tuned backbone can adapt to new statistics; a frozen one inherits the entire input contract." },

## Worked example — how much data does the head need?

<PredictReveal
  prompt="Freeze a ResNet-50 and train a linear head for 10 classes on its 2048-dim features. Fully fine-tuning the whole network trains roughly 25.6 million parameters. Roughly how many parameters does the linear head alone need?"
  options={["~200", "~20 thousand", "~2 million"]}
  correct={1}
>

Freeze a ResNet-50 and train a linear head for 10 classes on its 2048-dim features:

$$\text{params} = 2048 \times 10 + 10 = \mathbf{20{,}490}$$

Against fine-tuning the whole network:

$$25{,}600{,}000 + 20{,}490 \approx \mathbf{25.6M}$$

A **1,250× difference in the number of things your labels have to determine**. With 500 images the first is a comfortable ratio and the second is hopeless — which is the entire feature-extraction-versus-fine-tuning decision, made quantitative.

</PredictReveal>

<ConceptBox title="TL;DR">
- Transfer learning works because the **early layers of a vision model are nearly task-independent**. An edge detector is an edge detector whatever you photograph.
- Transferability **decays with depth**, and it decays faster the further your domain sits from ImageNet. So the question is never "transfer or not" — it is *how deep do I keep*.
- The single biggest predictor of success is **domain distance**, not dataset size. 5,000 pet photos transfer better than 50,000 histology slides.
- **Negative transfer is real**: on a distant domain a frozen deep backbone can be *worse* than training a small model from scratch.
- Reuse the **pretraining normalisation statistics**, or every frozen filter sees inputs off the distribution it was calibrated on.
</ConceptBox>
