# Module 1: What Hallucination Means in a GenAI Context

<Bridge>
Every previous track in this curriculum asked how to make a model work. This one asks what happens when it works exactly as designed and still produces something false — and why that is a predictable property of the design rather than a defect in it.
</Bridge>

## Fluency and truth are separate systems

<PredictReveal
  prompt="A language model produces a fluent, well-structured, confident paragraph containing a fabricated statistic. Which part of its training objective was violated?"
  options={["The factual-accuracy term — the model failed at what it was optimised for", "None of it — the objective never contained a term for truth in the first place"]}
  correct={1}
>

The training objective covered in the Transformers track is next-token prediction: given a context, assign high probability to plausible continuations. Nothing in that loss function evaluates whether a claim corresponds to reality. A model that produces a fluent fabrication has not failed at its objective — it has succeeded at it, on an input where the fluent continuation and the true one diverge.

This is why the framing matters. **Hallucination** is the standard term for output that is fluent and confident but unsupported — either false about the world, or unsupported by the sources the model was given. It is a term of art, and a somewhat unfortunate one: it borrows a word implying perception gone wrong, when the underlying process is the same statistical continuation that produces correct answers.

</PredictReveal>

## What hallucination is not

<PredictReveal
  prompt="Is a hallucination best understood as a bug — something that could in principle be patched out of the system with enough engineering?"
  options={["Yes — it is a defect in the implementation, fixable like any other bug", "No — it is a structural property of generating text from a probability distribution, which can be reduced and managed but not patched away"]}
  correct={1}
>

Three distinctions worth being precise about:

- **Not lying.** Lying requires knowing the truth and choosing to state otherwise. The model has no separate internal store of "what is true" that it is contradicting.
- **Not a bug.** A bug is a defect in an implementation that behaves differently from its specification. Hallucination arises *from* the specification: sample plausible text. There is no line of code to fix.
- **Not random noise.** Hallucinations are typically coherent, internally consistent, and plausible — that is exactly what makes them hard to spot and dangerous downstream.

The practical consequence runs through the rest of this half of the track: hallucination is a failure *rate* to be measured, reduced, and designed around, not a defect to be eliminated. Module 6 covers what that looks like in production.

</PredictReveal>

<ConceptBox title="TL;DR">
- **Hallucination** is fluent, confident output that is false about the world or unsupported by the provided sources.
- It does not violate the training objective — next-token prediction rewards plausibility and contains **no term for truth**, so a fluent fabrication is that objective working as specified.
- It is not lying (no internal truth being contradicted), not a bug (no departure from spec), and not noise (outputs are coherent and plausible, which is what makes them dangerous).
- The practical framing for everything that follows: a failure **rate** to measure and manage, not a defect to eliminate.
</ConceptBox>
