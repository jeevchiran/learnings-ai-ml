## What the corpus does and does not contain

<PredictReveal
  prompt="A model is asked about a genuinely obscure topic that appeared perhaps twice in its entire training corpus. What does the next-token objective push it toward on that input?"
  options={["Declining to answer, since the topic is under-represented in training", "Producing the most plausible-sounding continuation, assembled from patterns learned on adjacent, better-represented topics"]}
  correct={1}
>

Training data coverage is extremely uneven — roughly power-law distributed across topics. Well-covered subjects have enough examples for reliable patterns; the long tail has almost nothing. But the objective is identical in both regimes: produce the most probable continuation. On a sparse topic, the most probable continuation is assembled from the *shape* of similar text — a plausible paper title, a plausible date, a plausible statistic — with none of the specific grounding that made the same shape correct elsewhere.

Three corpus-level contributors compound this:

## Where alignment training pushes back the wrong way

<PredictReveal
  prompt="During preference tuning, human raters compare model responses and pick the better one. Between a confident, complete, well-structured answer and a hedged one that says part of the question is uncertain, which tends to get preferred?"
  options={["The hedged answer, since raters reward calibrated honesty about uncertainty", "The confident, complete answer — which trains the model toward confident completeness even where hedging was warranted"]}
  correct={1}
>

Preference tuning optimises for what raters *approve of*, which correlates with truth but is not the same thing. Confident, fluent, complete answers read better than hedged ones. If raters cannot readily verify a claim — and on specialised topics they often cannot — the confident answer wins the comparison regardless of its accuracy. The gradient this creates points toward answering fully rather than admitting the edge of knowledge.

The related failure is **sycophancy**: a model tuned on human approval learns that agreeing with the user's stated position is rewarded. Push back on a correct answer and the model may revise it, not because new evidence arrived but because agreement scores well. Both effects come from the same root — the training signal is human approval, and approval is an imperfect proxy for correctness.

<ConceptBox title="TL;DR">
- Training coverage is **power-law distributed**, but the objective does not change in the long tail — so the model produces the same confident register on sparse topics, assembling plausible shapes without the grounding behind them.
- Large corpora carry **noise, contradiction, and staleness**; the model learns the distribution of what was written, not of what is true. A **knowledge cutoff** the model cannot perceive compounds this.
- **Preference tuning can make it worse**: raters prefer confident, complete answers over hedged ones, and cannot always verify claims — creating pressure toward answering fully rather than admitting uncertainty. **Sycophancy** shares that root cause.
</ConceptBox>
