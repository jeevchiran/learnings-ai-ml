## A diffusion model is a hierarchical VAE

<PredictReveal
  prompt="A VAE has one latent variable z and one encoder/decoder pair, trained by maximizing an ELBO. A diffusion model has T latent variables x_1...x_T and a fixed 'encoder' (the forward process) with a learned 'decoder' (the reverse process). Does the same ELBO machinery apply?"
  options={["No — diffusion models require an entirely different objective, unrelated to the ELBO", "Yes — a diffusion model's training objective is a variational lower bound on log p(x_0), exactly the ELBO idea extended to a chain of T latent variables"]}
  correct={1}
>

The forward process plays the role of the VAE's fixed approximate posterior $q(z|x)$ — except here it has no learned parameters at all, and it is a chain of $T$ steps instead of one. The reverse process plays the role of the decoder $p_\theta(x|z)$. Maximizing a variational lower bound on $\log p(x_0)$ in this setting decomposes into a sum of $T$ terms, each one a KL divergence between the true (small) reverse step and the model's approximation of it — structurally the same ELBO decomposition from the VAE track, just with $T$ latent variables chained together instead of one.

</PredictReveal>

## The bound simplifies to a single MSE loss

<PredictReveal
  prompt="Since each term in the decomposed bound is a KL divergence between two Gaussians (the true reverse step and the model's Gaussian approximation), and KL divergence between two Gaussians has a closed form (from the VAE track's analytical KL derivation) — does the final training loss end up looking like the VAE's complicated ELBO, or something simpler?"
  options={["It stays as complicated as the full ELBO — no further simplification is possible", "It simplifies dramatically to a simple mean-squared-error loss between the true noise ε and the network's predicted noise"]}
  correct={1}
>

Because both distributions being compared are Gaussian, and because $\mu_\theta$ can be reparameterized in terms of predicting the noise $\epsilon$ directly (rather than predicting $x_{t-1}$'s mean directly), the KL terms collapse to a simple, re-weighted squared-error form. The DDPM paper found that dropping the theoretically-derived weighting entirely and just using unweighted MSE works better in practice:

$$L_{\text{simple}} = \mathbb{E}_{t, x_0, \epsilon}\Big[\big\| \epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \big\|^2\Big]$$

Train by picking a random $x_0$, a random timestep $t$, a random noise $\epsilon$, forming $x_t$ with Module 2's closed form, and asking the network to predict $\epsilon$ from $x_t$ and $t$. No adversary, no discriminator, no KL annealing schedule to babysit — just regression.

</PredictReveal>

Training is an ordinary mean-squared-error regression between the true noise and the predicted noise, with no adversarial component." },

## Optional depth — how the bound collapses to noise prediction

"It simplifies to an MSE on the noise" is a large claim to accept on trust. Here is the shape of the argument; the main thread does not depend on following it.

**Step 1 — the bound becomes a sum of Gaussian KLs.** Expanding the variational bound over the chain gives one term per timestep, each comparing the true reverse step against the model's:

$$D_{KL}\big(q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) \,\|\, p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)\big)$$

Note the true reverse step is conditioned on $x_0$. That extra conditioning is what makes it tractable — unlike $q(x_{t-1}|x_t)$ alone, it has a closed-form Gaussian solution with a known mean $\tilde{\mu}_t(x_t, x_0)$.

**Step 2 — Gaussian KL is a squared distance between means.** Both distributions are Gaussian, and DDPM fixes the reverse variance rather than learning it. The Gaussian KL formula (VAE track, Module 5) then reduces to a scaled squared difference of the means, with everything else constant:

$$D_{KL} = \frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\big\| \tilde{\mu}_t(x_t, x_0) - \mu_\theta(x_t, t) \big\|^2 + \text{const}$$

**Step 3 — rewrite the target mean in terms of the noise.** Module 2's closed form, $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon$, can be rearranged to express $x_0$ using $x_t$ and $\epsilon$. Substituting that into $\tilde{\mu}_t$ gives a mean written in terms of quantities the network already has ($x_t$, $t$) plus the one thing it does not: the noise $\epsilon$.

**Step 4 — parameterise the model the same way.** Since $\mu_\theta$ only has to match $\tilde\mu_t$, give it the identical algebraic form but with a predicted $\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$ in place of the true $\epsilon$. Every shared term then cancels in the subtraction, and the squared difference of means becomes a squared difference of *noises*:

$$\big\| \tilde{\mu}_t - \mu_\theta \big\|^2 \;\propto\; \big\| \epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \big\|^2$$

**Step 5 — drop the weights.** Each timestep's term carries a coefficient depending on $\bar\alpha_t$ and $\sigma_t$. Ho et al. found that simply setting every coefficient to 1 trains better than the theoretically-derived weighting — it up-weights the harder, noisier timesteps — leaving:

$$L_{\text{simple}} = \mathbb{E}_{t, x_0, \epsilon}\Big[\big\| \epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \big\|^2\Big]$$

So the final loss is not an approximation of the bound but a deliberately **re-weighted** version of it: the same per-timestep terms, weighted for training convenience rather than for tightness.

---

<ConceptBox title="TL;DR">
- A diffusion model is a **hierarchical VAE**: the fixed forward process plays the encoder's role (with no learned parameters), the learned reverse process plays the decoder's role, and training maximizes a variational lower bound on $\log p(x_0)$ — the same ELBO idea from the VAE track, chained across $T$ steps.
- Because every term compares two Gaussians, the bound collapses to a closed-form KL, which further simplifies (via the noise-prediction reparameterization) to an ordinary regression loss.
- The **simple loss** $L_{\text{simple}} = \mathbb{E}[\|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\|^2]$ just asks the network to predict the noise added at a random timestep — no adversarial component, no annealing schedule.
</ConceptBox>
