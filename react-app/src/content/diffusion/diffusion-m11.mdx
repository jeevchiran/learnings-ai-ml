## The trilogy, side by side

<PredictReveal

  correct={0}
>

VAEs and diffusion models are close cousins: both maximize a variational lower bound on $\log p(x)$, and Module 4 showed a diffusion model is literally a hierarchical VAE with a fixed, multi-step encoder. GANs stand apart: no likelihood bound at all, just an adversarial classification game between two networks (GAN track, Module 1). That's the deepest dividing line across the three — not sample quality, not architecture, but whether the training signal comes from a likelihood-style bound or from fooling a classifier.

## Why diffusion won on quality despite being slow

<PredictReveal
  prompt="Diffusion models need hundreds to thousands of sequential steps to sample (Module 6), while a GAN needs one pass. Given that speed disadvantage, why did diffusion models come to dominate large-scale image generation over GANs?"
  options={["Sampling speed turned out not to matter in practice for any application", "Training stability at scale mattered more: a diffusion model's plain regression loss scales predictably with more data and compute, where GAN training's adversarial dynamics become harder to stabilize as model and dataset size grow"]}

>

GAN training's saddle-point search (GAN track, Module 2) becomes harder to keep stable as networks and datasets scale up — the same mode-collapse and vanishing-gradient issues from the GAN track's early modules don't just disappear at scale, and often get worse. A diffusion model's simple noise-prediction loss (Module 4) is ordinary supervised regression, which scales the way most deep learning does: more data and compute reliably help, without a second network's dynamics to destabilize training. Techniques like DDIM (Module 7) and latent diffusion (Module 9) then closed most of the speed gap, leaving diffusion's stability advantage as the deciding factor for large, expensive training runs.

## Where each still wins

VAEs remain the fastest and most stable choice when an explicit, invertible encoder is genuinely needed — anomaly detection, representation learning, or any pipeline that has to map data into a latent code, not just out of one. GANs remain relevant for real-time or resource-constrained generation and for CycleGAN-style unpaired translation, where diffusion's per-sample cost is a harder constraint to work around. Diffusion models lead wherever sample fidelity and training stability at scale matter more than sampling latency — which describes most of today's large text-to-image and video generation systems.
