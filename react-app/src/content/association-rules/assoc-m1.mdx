<ConceptBox title="TL;DR">
- Unsupervised, but not clustering: we group **items that co-occur**, not customers.
- Three numbers judge a rule $A\Rightarrow B$: **support** = how often (is it worth acting on?), **confidence** = $P(B\mid A)$ (how reliable?), **lift** = $P(B\mid A)/P(B)$ (is it real?).
- **Lift is the honest one.** Confidence alone rewards popular consequents — a rule can hit 0.9 confidence purely because $B$ is in most baskets.
- Read lift as: $>1$ positive association, $=1$ independent, $<1$ negative.
</ConceptBox>

## Why Association Rules?

A supermarket has millions of receipts and one nagging question: **what gets bought together?** Nobody labeled these baskets — there is no target column, no "correct" answer to predict. This is unsupervised learning, but not clustering: we are not grouping *customers*, we are finding *items that co-occur*. The output is a set of human-readable rules like `{Diaper} ⇒ {Beer}` — "customers who buy diapers tend to buy beer" — that a merchandiser can act on directly.

> **Not prediction, not clustering.** Association rule mining discovers *co-occurrence patterns* in transactions. The famous (if apocryphal) "beer and diapers" story — young fathers grabbing both on a late store run — is the canonical example of a rule no one thought to look for.

Where it delivers value:

| Domain | What a rule captures |
|---|---|
| Retail / e-commerce | "bought together" → shelf layout, bundle offers, `also bought` widgets |
| Web usage | pages visited in the same session → navigation redesign |
| Healthcare | symptoms / diagnoses co-occurring → clinical decision support |
| Fraud / security | events firing together in one session → alert rules |
| Bioinformatics | genes co-expressed across conditions |

## Three Numbers That Define a Rule

A rule $A \Rightarrow B$ (antecedent $A$, consequent $B$, with $A \cap B = \emptyset$) is judged by three metrics.

<DerivationSteps>
<DerivationStep number={1} title="Support — how common is the pattern?">
$$\text{support}(A) = \frac{\text{transactions containing } A}{N}$$
The fraction of *all* baskets that contain the itemset. It measures how *frequent* — and therefore how *worth acting on* — a pattern is. A rule about an itemset bought twice in a million receipts is statistically noise.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={2} title="Confidence — how reliable is the implication?">
$$\text{confidence}(A \Rightarrow B) = \frac{\text{support}(A \cup B)}{\text{support}(A)} = P(B \mid A)$$
Of the baskets that have $A$, what fraction also have $B$? This is the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={3} title="Lift — is the association real?">
$$\text{lift}(A \Rightarrow B) = \frac{\text{confidence}(A \Rightarrow B)}{\text{support}(B)} = \frac{P(B \mid A)}{P(B)}$$
Confidence compared against $B$'s *baseline* rate. Lift $> 1$: $A$ makes $B$ **more** likely than average (positive association). Lift $= 1$: independent. Lift $< 1$: $A$ makes $B$ **less** likely.
</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

## Worked Example

Take the rule $\{Milk\} \Rightarrow \{Diaper\}$ on the database above. Milk appears in 8 baskets, Diaper in 7, both together in 6.

$$\text{support} = \tfrac{6}{10} = 0.60, \quad \text{confidence} = \tfrac{6}{8} = 0.75, \quad \text{lift} = \tfrac{0.75}{0.70} \approx 1.07$$

High support, high confidence, lift just above 1 — a common pairing but only a *slight* real association, because both items are already so popular.

<ConceptBox title="Common confusion — confidence is not enough">
Consider $\{Beer\} \Rightarrow \{Milk\}$: Beer in 7 baskets, both in 5, so confidence $= \tfrac{5}{7} \approx 0.71$ — looks strong! But Milk's baseline support is 0.80, so lift $= \tfrac{0.71}{0.80} \approx 0.89 < 1$. Beer buyers are actually **less** likely than average to buy milk. High confidence just reflects that milk is *everywhere*. **Always check lift** — confidence alone favors popular consequents.
</ConceptBox>

## Practice

**Problem 1 — Support.** What is the support of the itemset $\{Bread, Diaper\}$?

**Solution:** Baskets with both Bread and Diaper: #2, #4, #5, #8, #10 → 5 baskets. support $= 5/10 = 0.50$.

**Problem 2 — Confidence.** Compute confidence of $\{Diaper\} \Rightarrow \{Beer\}$.

**Solution:** Diaper in 7 baskets; Diaper+Beer in #2,#3,#4,#6,#10 → 5. confidence $= 5/7 \approx 0.71$.

**Problem 3 — Lift and interpretation.** For $\{Diaper\} \Rightarrow \{Beer\}$, Beer's support is 0.70. Lift?

**Solution:** lift $= 0.71 / 0.70 \approx 1.02$. Essentially **independent** — diapers and beer co-occur at almost exactly the chance rate here, despite the folklore. A lesson in checking your data before trusting a story.
