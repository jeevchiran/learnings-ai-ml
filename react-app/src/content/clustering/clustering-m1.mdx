<ConceptBox title="TL;DR">
- K-means alternates two steps: **assign** each point to its nearest centroid, then **move** each centroid to its group's mean. Repeat until nobody switches.
- Neither step can raise WCSS and there are finitely many assignments — so it **always terminates**.
- But it converges to a **local** minimum that depends on where you started. Hence `n_init`: run it several times, keep the lowest WCSS.
- **K-means++** seeds centroids with probability proportional to $D^2$, so far-away regions get represented — and buys an $O(\log K)$ approximation guarantee random init cannot offer.
- $K$ is a hyperparameter. The algorithm will never tell you it's wrong.
</ConceptBox>

## Why Clustering?

Supervised learning needs labels — a spam filter knows which emails are spam. But labels are expensive: annotating a million transactions as fraud/legit takes reviewers, legal review, and days. **Unsupervised learning** finds structure *without labels*, and clustering is its most-used technique. Instead of predicting a target, it asks: which points are similar? Group similar together, keep dissimilar apart — and structure emerges that no one thought to label.

> **Supervised vs. unsupervised:** supervised optimizes a loss against known labels; unsupervised clustering must define and optimize "good grouping" from the data alone. Evaluation is harder — but it can discover patterns humans never anticipated.

Where it delivers value:

| Domain | What clustering finds |
|---|---|
| Customer segmentation | loyalists, bargain hunters, seasonal shoppers → targeted marketing |
| Image compression | replace 16M colors with K=256 centroids (JPEG/PNG idea) |
| Document grouping | articles by topic with no pre-labeled categories |
| Anomaly detection | points far from every centroid — intrusions, defects, faults |
| Gene expression | co-regulated genes across conditions |
| City planning | neighborhoods by demographics → allocate schools, clinics |

## Clustering, Formally

Given $n$ points $\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_n \in \mathbb{R}^p$, a **clustering with K clusters** partitions $\{1,\ldots,n\}$ into K disjoint non-empty subsets:

$$C_1, \ldots, C_K \quad\text{with}\quad C_k \cap C_j = \emptyset,\;\; \bigcup_k C_k = \{1,\ldots,n\}$$

Points within a cluster should be similar, across clusters dissimilar. That raises four questions defining the field:

<ConceptBox title="Four fundamental questions">
1. **What is "similar"?** A distance measure — usually Euclidean (Module 2).
2. **How good is a clustering?** An objective — Within-Cluster Sum of Squares (Module 3).
3. **How do we find the best one?** Optimal partitioning is NP-hard; K-means approximates it (*this module*).
4. **How do we evaluate without labels?** Silhouette and the elbow method (Module 4).
</ConceptBox>

## K-Means — Intuition First

You walk into a room of 100 people and must form 3 discussion groups, knowing nothing:

1. Tap 3 people to be initial "leaders" — the *centroids*.
2. Everyone walks to their nearest leader — the *assignment step*.
3. Each leader moves to the geographic center of their group — the *update step*.
4. Some people are now closer to a different leader; they switch. Repeat from 3.
5. When nobody moves, it has *converged*.

That's K-means exactly — leaders = centroids, "closest" = Euclidean distance, "center" = arithmetic mean. Formally:

<DerivationSteps>
<DerivationStep number={0} title="Choose K">
K is a hyperparameter — the algorithm doesn't pick it (Modules 3–4).
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={1} title="Initialize K centroids">
Pick $\boldsymbol{\mu}_1, \ldots, \boldsymbol{\mu}_K$ — random points, or K-means++ (below).
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={2} title="Assignment step">
$$c_i = \underset{k}{\arg\min} \;\|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k\|^2$$
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={3} title="Update step">
$$\boldsymbol{\mu}_k = \frac{1}{|C_k|} \sum_{i:\, c_i = k} \mathbf{x}_i$$
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={4} title="Check convergence">
If any centroid moved, return to step 2; else stop (a tolerance or max-iteration cap is also used).
</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

It alternates optimizing assignments (centroids fixed) and centroids (assignments fixed). Each step can only lower or hold the cost — never raise it — which is why it always terminates.

## Convergence

Why does it stop? Three parts. The objective is the **Within-Cluster Sum of Squares**:

$$\text{WCSS} = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \sum_{i:\, c_i = k} \|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k\|^2$$

1. **Neither step increases WCSS** — assignment moves points to a strictly closer centroid; update sets each centroid to the mean, which minimizes squared distance within a set.
2. **Finitely many states** — at most $K^n$ assignments, huge but finite.
3. **So it terminates** — non-increasing WCSS over finitely many states can't cycle; it halts when no point wants to switch.

> **Local, not global minimum:** convergence is guaranteed, optimality is not. K-means finds a *local* WCSS minimum that depends on initialization. Remedy: run several times with different seeds, keep the lowest WCSS — scikit-learn's `n_init` (default 10).

> **Time complexity:** each iteration is $O(nKp)$; iterations to converge are usually few (10–100), so it scales to millions of points when K and p are modest.

## K-Means++ Initialization

Uniform-random init can place two centroids in the same dense region while a real cluster gets none → a poor local minimum. **K-means++** (Arthur & Vassilvitskii, 2007) spreads them probabilistically:

<DerivationSteps>
<DerivationStep number={1} title="First centroid: uniform random">
Every point equally likely.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={2} title="Subsequent: D²-proportional sampling">
Let $D(\mathbf{x}_i)$ = distance to the nearest chosen centroid. Sample the next with
$$P(\mathbf{x}_i) = \frac{D(\mathbf{x}_i)^2}{\sum_j D(\mathbf{x}_j)^2}$$
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={3} title="Repeat until K chosen">
Recompute $D^2$ against all chosen centroids, sample again, then run standard K-means.
</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

Points far from existing centroids get large $D^2$ and are likely picked next, spreading the seeds so no cluster is left unrepresented.

<ConceptBox title="Guarantee">
K-means++ gives expected WCSS at most $O(\log K)$ times optimal — impossible to state for random init, and in practice usually far better than the bound. All major libraries default to it: `KMeans(init='k-means++')`.
</ConceptBox>

## Practice

**Problem 1 — Assignment by hand.** Centroids $\boldsymbol{\mu}_1=(1,1)$, $\boldsymbol{\mu}_2=(5,5)$, $\boldsymbol{\mu}_3=(9,9)$. Assign $\mathbf{x}=(4,5)$.

**Solution:** $d_1 = \sqrt{9+16} = 5$, $d_2 = \sqrt{1+0} = 1$, $d_3 = \sqrt{25+16} \approx 6.4$. Minimum is $d_2$ → **Cluster 2**.

**Problem 2 — Update + reassignment.** Cluster 1 has $(2,3)$, $(4,1)$, $(3,5)$. (a) New centroid? (b) Should $(3.4, 2.8)$, currently in Cluster 2 (centroid $(1,1)$), move?

**Solution:** (a) $\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 = \left(\frac{2+4+3}{3}, \frac{3+1+5}{3}\right) = (3,3)$. (b) To $(3,3)$: $\sqrt{0.16+0.04} \approx 0.447$; to $(1,1)$: $\sqrt{5.76+3.24} = 3.0$. Since $0.447 < 3.0$, **reassign to Cluster 1** — why assignment repeats after each update.

**Problem 3 — K-means++ sampling.** First centroid at $A=(2,2)$. Candidates B=(2,3), C=(8,8), D=(9,7), E=(8,9). Squared distances and probabilities?

**Solution:** $D^2$: B=1, C=72, D=74, E=85; total 232. $P(B)\approx0.4\%$, $P(C)\approx31\%$, $P(D)\approx32\%$, $P(E)\approx36.6\%$. Most likely: **E**. The far cluster {C,D,E} gets ~99.5% combined vs nearby B's 0.5%, so the second centroid almost certainly lands in the far group — exactly the spread K-means++ is designed for.
