<ConceptBox title="TL;DR">
- The metric decides the cluster **shape** the algorithm can find: Euclidean → round, Manhattan → diamond, cosine → angular sector.
- **Metric and centroid update are one decision, not two.** Squared Euclidean ⟹ the mean is the exact minimiser; L1 ⟹ the median (K-medians). Swapping the metric alone breaks convergence.
- Cosine ignores magnitude, which is what you want for documents — but it doesn't drop into standard K-means without also changing the update.
- **Always Z-score before K-means.** Euclidean distance is not scale-invariant, so an unscaled income column will cluster your data by units, not structure.
</ConceptBox>

## Why Distance?

<Bridge>
K-means assigns each point to its "nearest" centroid — but nearest by what measure? The distance metric determines the cluster shapes the algorithm can find and what the centroid update means.
</Bridge>

Two points (2,3) and (5,7): how far apart? The straight-line (Euclidean) distance is one answer, but different problems call for different notions. A Manhattan taxi can't cut diagonally — it travels the grid. Two documents compare better by the *angle* between term-frequency vectors than by magnitude. **The metric should match the problem.**

<ConceptBox title="Three questions a metric answers">
- **Shape** — what do equidistant sets ("circles") look like? Round (Euclidean), diamond (Manhattan), sector (cosine)?
- **Centroid** — what point minimizes total distance? Mean (Euclidean), median (Manhattan)?
- **Scale sensitivity** — does rescaling a feature change which points are nearest?
</ConceptBox>

## Euclidean Distance

From the Pythagorean theorem, generalized to $p$ dimensions:

$$d_E(a, b) = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{p} (a_j - b_j)^2} = \|a - b\|_2$$

The **L2 norm** — K-means' default. Example: $d_E((1,4),(4,8)) = \sqrt{3^2+4^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5$ (a 3-4-5 triangle).

<ConceptBox title="Properties">
Symmetric; non-negative (zero iff $a=b$); triangle inequality; rotation-invariant. **NOT scale-invariant** — larger-range features dominate, so always standardize before K-means.
</ConceptBox>

## Manhattan Distance

The L1 / taxicab norm — sum of absolute coordinate differences:

$$d_M(a, b) = \sum_{j=1}^{p} |a_j - b_j| = \|a - b\|_1$$

For $(1,4)$ and $(4,8)$: $d_M = 3 + 4 = 7 > 5 = d_E$. The straight line is always shortest, so $d_E \leq d_M$.

<ConceptBox title="When to use Manhattan">
- **Less outlier-sensitive** — absolute values don't amplify large gaps the way squaring does.
- **Diamond-shaped balls** instead of circles.
- **Sparse high-dimensional data** (text, genomics) — curse of dimensionality is milder under L1.
- **Minimizer is the median** — L1 in K-means gives coordinate-wise median updates (K-medians).
</ConceptBox>

## Cosine Similarity

For text and sparse high-dimensional vectors, *direction* matters more than magnitude — a 10-word and a 1000-word document on the same topic should be "close." Cosine similarity is the cosine of the angle:

$$\cos(\theta) = \frac{a \cdot b}{\|a\|_2\, \|b\|_2}$$

Range $[-1,1]$: 1 = same direction, 0 = orthogonal, −1 = opposite. Cosine *distance* $= 1 - \cos(\theta) \in [0,2]$.

Example: $A=(1,2)$, $B=(2,4)$ (= 2A). $d_E \approx 2.24$ but $\cos\theta = \frac{10}{\sqrt5\cdot\sqrt{20}} = 1.0$, so cosine distance = 0. Which is "correct" depends on the problem.

> **Cosine and K-means don't mix cleanly.** Euclidean K-means uses the arithmetic mean as the optimal update. For cosine, the optimal centroid is the mean of unit-normalized vectors, re-normalized. Swapping in cosine without changing the update breaks convergence to a true optimum.

## The Centroid Is the Mean

The centroid of $S = \{x_1,\ldots,x_n\}$ is the arithmetic mean $\mu = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_i$ — the balance point. Here's why it's the correct K-means update: it minimizes the within-cluster squared distance $f(\mu_k) = \sum_{i \in C_k} \|x_i - \mu_k\|^2$.

<DerivationSteps>
<DerivationStep number={1} title="Differentiate">
Dimensions separate, so per coordinate:
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial \mu_k} = -2 \sum_{i \in C_k} (x_i - \mu_k) = 0$$
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={2} title="Solve">
$$\sum_{i \in C_k} x_i = |C_k|\,\mu_k \;\Rightarrow\; \mu_k = \frac{1}{|C_k|}\sum_{i \in C_k} x_i$$
The mean is the exact minimizer.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={3} title="Confirm minimum">
$\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial \mu_k^2} = 2|C_k| > 0$ ✓
</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

> **Euclidean distance and the mean are one decision.** The derivation works *because* distances are squared, making $f$ a convex quadratic with the mean as unique minimizer. A different metric gives a different optimal centroid. For L1 in 1D, $g(\mu) = \sum|x_i - \mu|$ is piecewise linear with slope $\#\{x_i<\mu\} - \#\{x_i>\mu\}$ — zero when equal counts lie on each side, i.e. the **median** (→ K-medians).

## Feature Scaling

Because Euclidean distance squares differences, large-range features dominate. With "age" (0–80) and "income" (0–100,000), income dwarfs age in any distance. Fix by standardizing:

$$\text{Z-score: } x_j' = \frac{x_j - \bar{x}_j}{s_j} \qquad \text{Min-max: } x_j' = \frac{x_j - \min_j}{\max_j - \min_j}$$

Z-score gives mean 0, SD 1 per feature (preferred — min-max is outlier-sensitive).

> **Always scale before K-means** — otherwise it clusters by whichever feature has the largest range, a meaningless accident of units.

**Example:** A = (age 10, income 50,000), B = (age 70, income 51,000). Unscaled: $d_E = \sqrt{3600 + 1{,}000{,}000} \approx 1001.8$ — income swamps age. After Z-scoring (age $\bar x=40,s=20$; income $\bar x=50500,s=500$): $A'=(-1.5,-1.0)$, $B'=(1.5,1.0)$, $d_E = \sqrt{9+4} \approx 3.61$ — now both contribute.

## Practice

**Problem 1 — Distances.** $A=(3,4)$, $B=(7,1)$: $d_E = \sqrt{16+9} = 5$; $d_M = 4+3 = 7$. Manhattan is larger because the straight line is the shortest path, so $d_E \leq d_M$ always (from $\|v\|_2 \leq \|v\|_1$).

**Problem 2 — Centroid minimizes WCSS.** Points $(0,0)$, $(4,0)$, $(2,4)$. Centroid $\mu = (2, 4/3)$; its WCSS $\approx 18.67$ vs $\mu'=(3,2)$'s $13+5+5 = 23$. The true centroid wins.

**Problem 3 — L2 mean vs L1 median.** For $f(\mu) = \sum\|x_i-\mu\|^2$, expand around $\bar x$: cross term vanishes since $\sum(x_i-\bar x)=0$, giving $f(\mu) = \sum\|x_i-\bar x\|^2 + n\|\bar x - \mu\|^2$, minimized at $\mu = \bar x$. For $g(\mu) = \sum|x_i-\mu|$, the subgradient $\#\{x_i<\mu\} - \#\{x_i>\mu\} = 0$ at the **median**.
