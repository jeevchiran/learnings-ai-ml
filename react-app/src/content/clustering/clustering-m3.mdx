<Bridge>
**Building on Module 2:** updating a centroid to the mean of its points minimizes the sum of squared distances within that cluster. K-means does this across all K clusters at once. The single number it drives down is the **Within-Cluster Sum of Squares (WCSS)** — scikit-learn's **inertia**.
</Bridge>

## What K-Means Optimizes

For clusters $C_1,\ldots,C_K$ with centroids $\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_K$:

$$\text{WCSS} = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \sum_{i \in C_k} \|x_i - \mu_k\|^2$$

Each term is the squared distance from a point to its centroid; the total is within-cluster spread — we want it **small**. WCSS = 0 would mean every point sits on its centroid (perfect compactness); real data always has positive WCSS.

## K-Means as Coordinate Descent

K-means alternates two steps, each guaranteed not to increase WCSS — making it **coordinate descent** over two variable sets:

- **Assignment** (centroids fixed): $c_i = \arg\min_k \|x_i - \mu_k\|^2$. Any other assignment puts a point in a farther cluster, raising WCSS.
- **Update** (assignments fixed): $\mu_k = \frac{1}{|C_k|}\sum_{i \in C_k} x_i$. From Module 2, the mean uniquely minimizes within-cluster squared distance.

<DerivationSteps>
<DerivationStep number={1} title="WCSS ≥ 0">
A sum of squares, bounded below by zero.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={2} title="Both steps are non-increasing">
Assignment reassigns to the nearest centroid; update moves to the mean — each only lowers or holds WCSS.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={3} title="Convergence">
Non-increasing WCSS over finitely many assignments ($\leq K^n$) can't cycle → converges in finite iterations.
</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

<ConceptBox title="Local minima">
Coordinate descent finds a *local* WCSS minimum; global optimization is NP-hard. Different initializations give different solutions — use **multiple random restarts** (keep lowest WCSS). K-means++ reduces but doesn't eliminate this.
</ConceptBox>

## Properties of WCSS

- **Always ≥ 0**, zero only if points coincide with centroids (impossible for distinct data).
- **Decreases as K grows** — at $K = n$, WCSS = 0. So WCSS *alone* can't pick K; it always favors more clusters.
- **Scale-dependent** — doubling all values quadruples WCSS. Standardize first; don't compare WCSS across differently-scaled datasets.

**Why WCSS(K+1) ≤ WCSS(K):** split any cluster $C_k$ into $C_k^L, C_k^R$; the sub-means fit their points at least as well as the original centroid (since the mean minimizes squared distance), so total WCSS can't rise.

## The Elbow Method

Since WCSS always falls with K, look at the *rate* of decrease: plot WCSS vs K and find the "kink."

**Intuition:** with $K^*$ natural clusters, for $K < K^*$ each new cluster separates a genuinely different group → **large** WCSS drop; for $K > K^*$ you're splitting already-tight clusters → **small** drop. The transition is the elbow.

<ConceptBox title="Limits of the elbow">
1. **Ambiguous** — the curve is often smooth with no clear kink; people disagree.
2. **Subjective** — a visual judgment, not automated.
3. **Fails for non-spherical / uneven clusters.**
4. **Cohesion only** — WCSS ignores separation, so tight-but-overlapping clusters look fine.
5. **Costly** — run K-means for every K, ideally with restarts.
</ConceptBox>

## The Gap Statistic

Tibshirani et al. (2001): compare observed log-WCSS to what you'd expect under a null of *no* structure (uniform random data):

$$\text{Gap}(K) = \mathbb{E}^*\!\left[\log \text{WCSS}_{\text{ref}}(K)\right] - \log \text{WCSS}(K)$$

Genuine structure makes observed WCSS much lower than random → large gap; no structure → gap near zero. Choose the smallest K with $\text{Gap}(K) \geq \text{Gap}(K+1) - s_{K+1}$ (a one-standard-error rule guarding against too-large K). Steps: run K-means on the data and on B reference datasets (uniform over the bounding box), record log-WCSS, average the references, apply the criterion.

<ConceptBox title="Edge over the elbow">
The gap statistic can detect **no** structure: if Gap(K) ≈ 0 for all K, the data is essentially random. The elbow always returns *some* answer even for uniform data.
</ConceptBox>

## WCSS Decomposition

Total spread splits into within and between:

$$\text{TSS} = \text{WCSS} + \text{BCSS}$$

- **TSS** $= \sum_i \|x_i - \mu_{\text{global}}\|^2$ — total spread around the global mean, **constant** in K.
- **BCSS** $= \sum_k |C_k|\, \|\mu_k - \mu_{\text{global}}\|^2$ — how far centroids sit from the global mean, size-weighted.

As K rises, WCSS falls and BCSS rises (TSS fixed). A good K makes clusters tight (low WCSS) *and* far apart (high BCSS).
