## Naive Bayes in Context

Every classifier trades off assumptions, training cost, and expressive power. Naive Bayes sits at one extreme: strong assumptions, minimal training cost, no feature interactions. Here it is against the alternatives along the axes that decide which to reach for.

## Head-to-Head

| Property | Naive Bayes | Logistic Regression | Decision Tree | k-NN | SVM |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Family | Generative | Discriminative | Discriminative | Instance-based | Discriminative |
| Training | Counting | Gradient (MLE) | Greedy splitting | None (lazy) | Convex QP |
| Train speed | Very fast $O(Nn)$ | Moderate | Moderate | None | Slow on large $N$ |
| Predict speed | Very fast | Very fast | Fast | Slow $O(N)$/query | Fast |
| Feature interactions | None | Only if engineered | Learned | Implicit (distance) | Implicit (kernel) |
| Data efficiency (small $N$) | Excellent | Good | Poor | Poor | Good w/ regularization |
| High-dim sparse | Excellent | Good | Poor | Poor | Good |
| Interpretability | High | High | Very high | Low | Low |
| Probabilities | Poorly calibrated | Well calibrated | Leaf frequencies | Not natural | Needs calibration |

## vs. Logistic Regression

The most instructive pair — mathematically linked. For Gaussian NB with shared covariance, the posterior takes exactly the sigmoid-of-linear form logistic regression assumes: same functional form, fit two different ways.

The difference is what each optimizes. Logistic regression is **discriminative** — it maximizes label likelihood given features, tuning the boundary to minimize error. Naive Bayes is **generative** — it fits $P(\mathbf{x}\mid y)$, and the boundary falls out. Ng & Jordan (2001): NB reaches its (higher) asymptotic error faster, with fewer examples; logistic regression overtakes it with enough data because it isn't hobbled by independence. Hence NB is the standard small-data / large-vocabulary baseline; logistic regression wins once there's enough data to estimate interactions.

<ConceptBox title="Rule of thumb">
Small data, huge sparse features (text), fast baseline → **Naive Bayes**. Larger data, calibrated probabilities, want to add interaction terms → **logistic regression**. Suspect correlations distort predictions → try logistic regression; if it doesn't help, independence wasn't the bottleneck.
</ConceptBox>

## vs. Decision Trees

A tree learns interactions automatically (each split can depend on earlier ones); NB structurally cannot. Trees are strictly more expressive on data with real interactions — but high-variance and overfitting-prone on small data, whereas NB's independence assumption acts as an implicit regularizer, staying stable when data is scarce relative to features.

## vs. k-NN

k-NN assumes almost nothing and has no training phase — but every prediction scans (or indexes) the whole training set, and it degrades badly in high dimensions. NB pays the opposite cost: it commits to a likelihood family upfront, but that lets it summarize the training set into a handful of counts or (mean, variance) pairs, making prediction instant regardless of $N$.

## vs. SVM

An SVM finds the maximum-margin boundary, optionally kernelized — usually higher accuracy than NB when data is ample and interactions matter, but training scales poorly ($O(N^2)$–$O(N^3)$), and raw output is a distance, not a probability. NB gives up the accuracy ceiling for linear-scaling train/predict and free (if imperfect) probabilities.

<Bridge>
Naive Bayes is rarely the *most accurate* model — it is the fastest reasonable one, and often the best choice specifically when data is limited, features are numerous and sparse, or a fast, interpretable, incrementally-updatable baseline beats squeezing out the last accuracy points. Knowing exactly why it wins there, and loses elsewhere, is what understanding a classifier means.
</Bridge>

## Practice

#### Problem 1: Model Under a Data Constraint
200 labeled documents, 30,000-word vocabulary, 5 categories. Naive Bayes or linear-kernel SVM as first choice?

**Solution:** Naive Bayes. With 200 examples and 30,000 features, any model estimating interactions risks severe overfitting on so sparse a space. NB only estimates $n$ small per-feature distributions per class, degrading gracefully as $N$ shrinks relative to $n$ — the data-efficiency argument. A baseline to beat, not necessarily the final model.

#### Problem 2: When Naive Bayes Loses
Fraud = "high amount AND low account age" (neither feature alone predictive). With ample data, which model class is guaranteed to beat NB, and why?

**Solution:** Any model that represents interactions — decision tree, kernel SVM, or logistic regression with an amount×age term. The true rule is a conjunction of two features, which independence forbids NB from representing regardless of data volume. A structural blind spot, not a data shortage.
