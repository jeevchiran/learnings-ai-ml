<ConceptBox title="TL;DR">
- A probabilistic classifier estimates $P(y\mid\mathbf{x})$ and takes the arg-max — the **Bayes decision rule**, provably optimal if the probabilities were exact.
- **Prior** = belief before evidence (the base rate). **Posterior** = belief after. Everything here is machinery for that update.
- **Discriminative** models draw the boundary ($P(y\mid\mathbf{x})$ directly). **Generative** models build a per-class simulator ($P(\mathbf{x}\mid y)$ and $P(y)$) and let Bayes do the rest.
- Generative buys you extras: sampling, graceful handling of missing features by marginalising, and incremental updates.
</ConceptBox>

## Why Probabilistic Classification?

A spam filter can decide "spam or not" in several ways. A decision tree walks yes/no questions to a leaf. Logistic regression checks which side of a boundary the email falls on. Both give a label — neither directly answers the question a human actually asks: *given everything I know about this email, how likely is it to be spam?*

A probabilistic classifier answers that directly. For every class $y$ it estimates $P(y \mid \mathbf{x})$ — the probability of the class given the features $\mathbf{x}$ — then picks the largest:

$$\hat{y} = \arg\max_{y} \; P(y \mid \mathbf{x})$$

This is the **Bayes decision rule**, and it is provably optimal: if you knew $P(y \mid \mathbf{x})$ exactly, no rule could achieve lower average error. Every probabilistic classifier is an attempt to estimate $P(y \mid \mathbf{x})$ well from finite data.

<ConceptBox title="Posterior, prior — the names">
$P(y \mid \mathbf{x})$ is the **posterior** — belief *after* seeing the features. Before seeing any, your best guess is the base rate each class occurs at: the **prior**. Observing $\mathbf{x}$ updates prior → posterior. This whole track is machinery to compute that update correctly and cheaply.
</ConceptBox>

## A Weather-Forecaster Analogy

A forecaster estimating rain combines two things:

1. **Base rate** — it rains on 20% of days regardless: $P(\text{rain}) = 0.2$ (the prior).
2. **Today's evidence** — overcast sky, high humidity, dropping pressure — each more common before rain.

The forecaster doesn't discard the base rate just because it looks stormy, nor ignore the evidence. The base rate gets *updated* by the evidence into a posterior. That update step is exactly what Bayes' theorem formalizes.

## Generative vs. Discriminative

Two ways to model $P(y \mid \mathbf{x})$:

| Approach | What it models | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Discriminative** | $P(y \mid \mathbf{x})$ directly — a boundary or scoring function | Logistic regression, SVM, decision trees |
| **Generative** | $P(\mathbf{x} \mid y)$ and $P(y)$ separately, combined via Bayes' theorem | Naive Bayes, GDA, HMMs |

A generative model asks not "where is the boundary?" but "if I were to *generate* an example of class $y$, what would its features look like?" It builds a small simulator per class, and classification falls out of comparing how well each class's simulator explains $\mathbf{x}$. The payoff: because it represents $P(\mathbf{x}\mid y)$, it can also generate samples, handle missing features by marginalizing, and update incrementally.

Naive Bayes is the simplest generative classifier: each class's "simulator" is just a product of independent per-feature distributions. ## Practice

#### Problem 1: Prior vs. Posterior
1 in 500 ER patients have a rare condition (prior). A patient arrives with symptoms strongly associated with it. What should the doctor reason about?

**Solution:** The prior $P(\text{condition}) = 0.002$ is the base rate *before* symptoms. Once symptoms $\mathbf{x}$ are seen, the relevant quantity is the posterior $P(\text{condition} \mid \text{symptoms})$ — which can be far higher than the prior, but is never obtained by discarding it. A generative model keeps both base rate and symptom likelihood and combines them.

#### Problem 2: Generative vs. Discriminative
Classify each: (a) logistic regression, (b) Gaussian Naive Bayes, (c) decision tree, (d) linear discriminant analysis.

**Solution:** (a) Discriminative — models $P(y\mid\mathbf{x})$ via a sigmoid, no model of $\mathbf{x}$. (b) Generative — per-class Gaussian over $\mathbf{x}$ plus prior. (c) Discriminative — axis-aligned splits partition $\mathbf{x}$-space directly. (d) Generative — per-class Gaussian with shared covariance, then Bayes' theorem.
