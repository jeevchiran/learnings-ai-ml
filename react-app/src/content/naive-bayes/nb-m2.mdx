- The whole derivation is one move: **the joint can be written two ways**, $P(A\mid B)P(B)=P(B\mid A)P(A)$. Solve for what you want.
- Four terms worth naming: posterior, likelihood, prior, evidence. The **evidence is just a normaliser** built by the law of total probability.

## Conditional Probability

The probability of $A$ given $B$ occurred is *defined* as:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(B) > 0$$

Restrict attention to the world where $B$ is true, then ask what fraction of it also has $A$ true. Rearranging gives the **multiplication rule** $P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B)$; swapping roles gives $P(A \cap B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A)$. Both equal the same joint probability — that is the entire trick behind Bayes' theorem.

## Deriving Bayes' Theorem

<DerivationSteps>
<DerivationStep number={1} title="Two expressions for the joint">
$$P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\, P(B) \qquad\text{and}\qquad P(A \cap B) = P(B \mid A)\, P(A)$$
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={2} title="Set them equal">
$$P(A \mid B)\, P(B) = P(B \mid A)\, P(A)$$
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={3} title="Solve for P(A | B)">
$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\, P(A)}{P(B)}$$
It flips a conditional: knowing how likely evidence $B$ is *given* cause $A$, you recover how likely cause $A$ is *given* evidence $B$.
</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

<ConceptBox title="The four terms">
- **Posterior** $P(A \mid B)$ — updated belief after seeing $B$.
- **Likelihood** $P(B \mid A)$ — how probable the evidence is if $A$ were true.
- **Prior** $P(A)$ — belief before any evidence.
- **Evidence** $P(B)$ — total probability of $B$; a normalizing constant.
</ConceptBox>

## The Evidence Term

$P(B)$ is rarely known directly — build it from the pieces you have. With $A$ and $\bar A$ partitioning all cases, the **law of total probability** gives $P(B) = P(B \mid A)P(A) + P(B \mid \bar A)P(\bar A)$, or for $K$ classes:

$$P(A_i \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A_i)\, P(A_i)}{\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K} P(B \mid A_k)\, P(A_k)}$$

The denominator sums over *every* class, so it doesn't depend on which $A_i$ you score — a shared constant. That becomes the key shortcut in the next module.

## Worked Example: The Medical-Test Paradox

Disease prevalence $P(D) = 0.01$. Test detects disease 99% of the time ($P(\text{pos}\mid D) = 0.99$), false-positives 5% of healthy people ($P(\text{pos}\mid \bar D) = 0.05$). A patient tests positive — what is $P(D \mid \text{pos})$?

<DerivationSteps>
<DerivationStep number={1} title="Evidence P(pos)">
$$P(\text{pos}) = (0.99)(0.01) + (0.05)(0.99) = 0.0099 + 0.0495 = 0.0594$$
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={2} title="Apply Bayes' theorem">
$$P(D \mid \text{pos}) = \frac{0.0099}{0.0594} \approx 0.167$$
</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

Despite a 99%-accurate test and a positive result, the patient's chance of disease is only **≈16.7%**. The disease is so rare that false positives (5% of the 99% healthy) outnumber true positives (99% of the 1% sick) roughly 5-to-1. A low prior cannot be ignored just because the evidence looks strong. ## Practice

#### Problem 1: Spam Filter Update
$P(\text{spam}) = 0.05$. "free" appears in 40% of spam, 2% of legit. An email contains "free". Find $P(\text{spam} \mid \text{"free"})$.

**Solution:**
$$P(\text{"free"}) = (0.40)(0.05) + (0.02)(0.95) = 0.039$$
$$P(\text{spam} \mid \text{"free"}) = \frac{0.02}{0.039} \approx 0.513$$
One word roughly doubles a 5% prior into 51.3% — a big update, still far from certainty.

#### Problem 2: Symmetry Check
Show $P(A\mid B)P(B) = P(B\mid A)P(A)$ implies $P(A \mid B)/P(B\mid A) = P(A)/P(B)$.

**Solution:** Divide both sides by $P(B\mid A)P(B)$: $\frac{P(A\mid B)}{P(B\mid A)} = \frac{P(A)}{P(B)}$. The relative pull of $A$ vs $B$ as the "given" mirrors how common $A$ is versus $B$ to begin with.
