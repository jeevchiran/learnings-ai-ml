## Conditional Probability, Precisely

The probability of event $A$ given that event $B$ has occurred is defined as:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(B) > 0$$

In words: restrict attention to the world where $B$ is true, then ask what fraction of that restricted world also has $A$ true. This is a *definition*, not a derived fact — everything else in this module follows from applying it twice.

Rearranging the definition gives the **multiplication rule**:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B) \, P(B)$$

By the same definition applied with the roles of $A$ and $B$ swapped:

$$P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad P(A \cap B) = P(B \mid A) \, P(A)$$

Both expressions equal the same quantity $P(A \cap B)$ — this is the entire trick behind Bayes' theorem.

## Deriving Bayes' Theorem

<DerivationSteps>
<DerivationStep number={1} title="Start from two expressions for the joint probability">
From the definition of conditional probability applied in both directions:
$$P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\, P(B) \qquad \text{and} \qquad P(A \cap B) = P(B \mid A)\, P(A)$$
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={2} title="Set the two expressions equal">
Both are the same joint probability $P(A \cap B)$, so:
$$P(A \mid B)\, P(B) = P(B \mid A)\, P(A)$$
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={3} title="Solve for P(A | B)">
Divide both sides by $P(B)$:
$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\, P(A)}{P(B)}$$
This is **Bayes' theorem**. It lets you flip the direction of a conditional probability: if you know how likely evidence $B$ is *given* a cause $A$, you can compute how likely cause $A$ is *given* the evidence $B$.
</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

<ConceptBox title="Naming the four terms">
- **Posterior** $P(A \mid B)$ — updated belief about $A$ after observing $B$.
- **Likelihood** $P(B \mid A)$ — how probable the evidence $B$ is, if $A$ were true.
- **Prior** $P(A)$ — belief about $A$ before observing any evidence.
- **Evidence / marginal** $P(B)$ — total probability of observing $B$ at all, under any value of $A$. Acts as a normalizing constant so the posterior sums to 1.
</ConceptBox>

## The Evidence Term via Law of Total Probability

$P(B)$ is rarely known directly — it has to be built from the same pieces you already have. If $A$ and its complement $\bar{A}$ partition all possibilities, the **law of total probability** gives:

$$P(B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A) + P(B \mid \bar{A})\,P(\bar{A})$$

More generally, if $A$ can take any of $K$ mutually exclusive values $A_1, \ldots, A_K$:

$$P(B) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} P(B \mid A_k)\, P(A_k)$$

Substituting this into Bayes' theorem gives the fully expanded form used throughout classification:

$$P(A_i \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A_i)\, P(A_i)}{\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K} P(B \mid A_k)\, P(A_k)}$$

The denominator sums over *every* possible class, so it does not depend on which class $A_i$ you're evaluating — it is a shared normalizing constant. This observation becomes the key computational shortcut in the next module.

## Worked Example: The Medical Test Paradox

A disease affects 1% of a population: $P(D) = 0.01$. A test correctly detects the disease 99% of the time ($P(\text{pos}\mid D) = 0.99$) but also gives a false positive on 5% of healthy people ($P(\text{pos}\mid \bar D) = 0.05$). A patient tests positive. What is $P(D \mid \text{pos})$?

<DerivationSteps>
<DerivationStep number={1} title="Compute the evidence term P(pos)">
$$P(\text{pos}) = P(\text{pos}\mid D)P(D) + P(\text{pos}\mid \bar D)P(\bar D) = (0.99)(0.01) + (0.05)(0.99) = 0.0099 + 0.0495 = 0.0594$$
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={2} title="Apply Bayes' theorem">
$$P(D \mid \text{pos}) = \frac{P(\text{pos}\mid D)\,P(D)}{P(\text{pos})} = \frac{0.0099}{0.0594} \approx 0.1667$$
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={3} title="Interpret the result">
Despite a 99%-accurate test and a positive result, the probability the patient actually has the disease is only about **16.7%** — not 99%. The disease is so rare (1% prior) that false positives from the large healthy population (5% of 99% of people) outnumber true positives from the tiny sick population (99% of 1% of people) by roughly 5 to 1. This is why a low prior cannot be ignored just because the evidence looks strong — Bayes' theorem forces you to weigh both.
</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

<Bridge>
**From two events to many features.** Bayes' theorem as derived here relates one event $A$ to one piece of evidence $B$. Classification needs to relate a class $y$ to an entire feature vector $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ simultaneously — email word counts, pixel values, sensor readings. Substituting $y$ for $A$ and $\mathbf{x}$ for $B$ gives $P(y \mid \mathbf{x}) \propto P(\mathbf{x} \mid y)\,P(y)$, but $P(\mathbf{x}\mid y)$ over many correlated features is generally intractable to estimate from limited data. The next module introduces the one assumption — conditional independence — that makes this tractable, and gives Naive Bayes its name.
</Bridge>

## Practice

#### Problem 1: Spam Filter Bayes Update

5% of all emails are spam: $P(\text{spam}) = 0.05$. The word "free" appears in 40% of spam emails and 2% of legitimate emails. An email contains "free". What is $P(\text{spam} \mid \text{"free"})$?

**Solution:**
$$P(\text{"free"}) = (0.40)(0.05) + (0.02)(0.95) = 0.02 + 0.019 = 0.039$$
$$P(\text{spam} \mid \text{"free"}) = \frac{0.02}{0.039} \approx 0.513$$
Seeing "free" roughly doubles a 5% prior into a 51.3% posterior — a huge update, but still nowhere near certainty from one word alone.

#### Problem 2: Symmetry Check

Show that $P(A\mid B)\,P(B) = P(B\mid A)\,P(A)$ implies $P(A \mid B) / P(B\mid A) = P(A)/P(B)$, and explain in words what this ratio means.

**Solution:** Dividing both sides of $P(A\mid B)P(B) = P(B\mid A)P(A)$ by $P(B\mid A)P(B)$ gives $\dfrac{P(A\mid B)}{P(B\mid A)} = \dfrac{P(A)}{P(B)}$. In words: how much more (or less) likely $A$ is than $B$ to be the "given" condition mirrors exactly how much more (or less) common $A$ is than $B$ in the first place. This is the algebraic seed from which every posterior-vs-prior comparison in this track grows.
