<ConceptBox title="TL;DR">
- **Jacobian** = first derivatives of a *vector*-valued function. **Hessian** = second derivatives of a *scalar*-valued one. The Hessian is the Jacobian of the gradient.
- The Hessian is curvature, and its **eigenvalues classify a flat point**: all positive → minimum, all negative → maximum, mixed → saddle.
- In high dimensions all-positive is improbable, so most critical points are saddles — this, not local minima, is what stalls deep-net training.
- Newton's method uses $H^{-1}\nabla f$ and converges faster but costs too much at network scale; Adam approximates the same information cheaply.
</ConceptBox>

## Beyond the First Derivative

The gradient (Module 9) tells you the slope. It says nothing about whether that slope is about to flatten out, keep going, or reverse — that's **curvature**, and it's the last piece needed to fully explain why gradient descent behaves the way it does.

<ConceptBox title="Jacobian vs. Hessian — don't mix them up">
- **Jacobian** — for a *vector-valued* function $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$, the matrix of all first-order partial derivatives, $J_{ij}=\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}$. This is the general object backprop computes layer-to-layer (each layer's output is a vector, not a scalar).
- **Hessian** — for a *scalar-valued* function $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$, the matrix of all **second**-order partial derivatives, $H_{ij}=\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i \partial x_j}$. This is curvature — the Jacobian of the gradient.
</ConceptBox>

## The Hessian, for a Quadratic

<DerivationSteps>
<DerivationStep number={1} title="Set up">
$$f(x,y) = ax^2+bxy+cy^2 \implies H = \begin{bmatrix}2a & b\\ b & 2c\end{bmatrix}$$
For this family the Hessian is *constant* everywhere — the curvature doesn't change as you move, which is exactly why it's a useful teaching example (and a reasonable local approximation of any smooth loss surface near a point, via a second-order Taylor expansion).
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={2} title="Read the eigenvalues (Module 3) of H">
Both eigenvalues positive → **convex bowl**, a genuine local minimum. Both negative → local maximum. **Mixed signs → saddle point** — curves up in one direction, down in another. This is the formal version of the "flat point" ambiguity from Module 9.
</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

<GradientDescentWidget defaultShape="Saddle point" />

<ConceptBox title="Common confusion — why deep learning worries more about saddles than local minima">
In high dimensions, a critical point ($\nabla f=0$) needs *every* eigenvalue positive to be a true local minimum — increasingly unlikely by chance as dimensions grow. Most critical points in a high-dimensional loss landscape are saddles, not minima, which is a big part of why plain gradient descent can stall and why momentum-based optimizers (which carry velocity through flat/saddle regions) tend to outperform it in practice.
</ConceptBox>

## Where This Shows Up in Practice

- **Newton's method** uses $H^{-1}\nabla f$ instead of just $\nabla f$ — it accounts for curvature directly, converging faster near a minimum, at the cost of computing (and inverting) the full Hessian, which is expensive at neural-network scale.
- **Adam and other adaptive optimizers** approximate curvature *cheaply*, per-parameter, without ever forming the full Hessian.
- **Second-order Taylor expansion**: $f(\theta+\Delta) \approx f(\theta) + \nabla f^\top \Delta + \tfrac12 \Delta^\top H \Delta$ — the Hessian is literally the quadratic term describing how the loss curves around your current point.

## In Python

```python
import torch

x = torch.tensor([1.0, 1.0], requires_grad=True)

def f(v):
    return v[0]**2 - v[1]**2   # saddle: eigenvalues [2, -2]

hessian = torch.autograd.functional.hessian(f, x)
print(hessian)
# tensor([[ 2.,  0.],
#         [ 0., -2.]])   -- matches the analytic [[2a,b],[b,2c]] formula, a=1,b=0,c=-1
```

## Practice

**Problem 1.** $f(x,y)=2x^2+2y^2$. Write the Hessian and classify the critical point at the origin.

**Solution:** $H=\begin{bmatrix}4&0\\0&4\end{bmatrix}$, eigenvalues both 4 (positive) → the origin is a local (here, global) minimum.

**Problem 2.** $f(x,y)=x^2-4y^2$. Same question.

**Solution:** $H=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&-8\end{bmatrix}$, eigenvalues 2 and -8 (mixed signs) → a saddle point, not a minimum.
