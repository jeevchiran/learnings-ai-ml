## Starting the Calculus Track

Vectors gave us space, probability gave us uncertainty — calculus gives us **optimization**: the machinery every training loop uses to turn "here's how wrong the model is" into "here's how to adjust it."

## The Derivative

$$f'(x) = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$$

The instantaneous rate of change — geometrically, the slope of the line tangent to $f$ at $x$. $f'(x)>0$: increasing. $f'(x)<0$: decreasing. $f'(x)=0$: flat — a candidate minimum, maximum, or saddle.

## Partial Derivatives & the Gradient

Real models have many parameters, not one. A **partial derivative** $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ treats every other variable as a constant and asks "how does $f$ change if only $x$ moves?" Stack all of them into a vector and you get the **gradient**:

$$\nabla f = \left[\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}, \dots, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}\right]$$

The gradient points in the direction of **steepest ascent** — which is exactly why gradient *descent* (Module 10) moves opposite to it.

<PredictReveal

  correct={1}
>

Steepest ascent reversed is steepest descent, which is the entire content of the minus sign in every training update you will ever write. Module 10 builds the rest of the loop around that single subtraction.

</PredictReveal>

## The Chain Rule

<DerivationSteps>
<DerivationStep number={1} title="Single variable">
$$\frac{d}{dx}f(g(x)) = f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x)$$
The derivative of a composition is the product of the derivatives of each piece — "how much does the outer function change per unit of inner function, times how much the inner function changes per unit of x."
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep number={2} title="Why this is backpropagation">
A neural network is a composition of layers: $\text{loss}(\dots f_3(f_2(f_1(x))))$. Backprop computes $\frac{\partial \text{loss}}{\partial w}$ for every weight $w$ buried inside that composition by repeatedly applying the chain rule, layer by layer, back to front — multiplying local derivatives along the way.
</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

<PredictReveal
  prompt="Backpropagation multiplies one local derivative per layer to reach an early weight. Sigmoid derivatives are at most 0.25. What happens to that product across forty layers?"
  options={["It stays roughly constant, since each factor is positive", "It shrinks towards nothing, so early layers barely learn"]}
  correct={1}
>

Multiplying forty numbers each below 1 drives the product towards zero exponentially in depth. That is the vanishing-gradient problem, and it is the single reason ReLU replaced sigmoid in deep networks. Factors above 1 produce the mirror-image failure, exploding gradients.

</PredictReveal>

<ConceptBox title="Common confusion — a zero gradient isn't always a minimum">
$f'(x)=0$ also happens at local maxima and saddle points. Distinguishing them needs the *second* derivative (Module 11) — positive curvature confirms a minimum, negative confirms a maximum, mixed signs (in multiple dimensions) mean a saddle.
</ConceptBox>

## In Python (Autodiff, not by hand)

```python
import torch

x = torch.tensor(2.0, requires_grad=True)
y = (x ** 3) - 3 * x          # f(x) = x^3 - 3x

y.backward()                   # computes dy/dx automatically via the chain rule
print(x.grad)                  # 9.0  ==  3*(2**2) - 3, the analytic derivative
```

PyTorch's `autograd` is literally the chain rule applied automatically through every operation you write — this is what makes training arbitrary architectures practical without hand-deriving gradients.

---

<ConceptBox title="TL;DR">
- A derivative is a slope; a **partial** derivative is the slope along one axis with the rest held still.
- Stack the partials into the **gradient** $\nabla f$ — it points along steepest *ascent*, which is why descent subtracts it.
- The **chain rule** multiplies the derivatives of composed functions. A network *is* a composition, so backprop is the chain rule run back-to-front.
- $f'(x)=0$ marks a flat point, not necessarily a minimum — maxima and saddles are flat too.
</ConceptBox>

## Practice

**Problem 1.** $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$. Find where $f'(x)=0$.

**Solution:** $f'(x) = 3x^2-3 = 0 \implies x=\pm1$ — two flat points (one local max, one local min; verified with the widget above).

**Problem 2.** $f(x) = (2x+1)^2$. Use the chain rule to find $f'(x)$.

**Solution:** Outer function $u^2$ (derivative $2u$), inner $u=2x+1$ (derivative 2). $f'(x) = 2(2x+1)\cdot2 = 4(2x+1) = 8x+4$.
