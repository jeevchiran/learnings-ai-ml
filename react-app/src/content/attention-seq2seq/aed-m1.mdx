# Module 1: Word Embeddings — Strengths and Limits

<Bridge>

A word embedding is a dense vector that stands in for a word — learned so that words used in similar contexts end up with similar vectors. "coffee" and "tea" land close together; "coffee" and "bicycle" don't. That single idea — distributional similarity, encoded as geometry — is what every model in this track builds on top of.

## One vector per word, learned from context

<PredictReveal
  prompt="word2vec trains by predicting a word from its neighbours (or vice versa) across a huge corpus. Two words that never once appear in the same sentence — can they still end up with similar vectors?"
  options={["No — similarity requires having appeared together", "Yes — sharing the same kinds of neighbours is enough"]}
  correct={1}
>

"cat" and "dog" rarely sit in the exact same sentence, but both show up near "pet", "vet", "feed", "walk". word2vec (skip-gram or CBOW) and GloVe both exploit this: predict a word from its context (or context from the word) over millions of sentences, and words with interchangeable contexts converge to nearby vectors — without ever being told what "cat" or "dog" mean.

The result is a lookup table: one fixed vector per vocabulary word, typically 100–300 dimensions, learned once and reused everywhere. Arithmetic on these vectors captures relationships too — the classic example is `king - man + woman ≈ queen`.

</PredictReveal>

## The limit: one vector, no matter the sentence

<PredictReveal
  prompt='"river bank" and "savings bank" both contain the word "bank". In a plain word-embedding table (word2vec/GloVe), do these two occurrences get the same vector or different vectors?'
  options={["The same vector", "Different vectors, based on context"]}
  correct={0}
>

A classic embedding table is a fixed lookup: `embedding["bank"]` returns one vector, always, regardless of what surrounds it in a given sentence. "bank" in "river bank" and "bank" in "savings bank" are the exact same 300 numbers. The model has folded every sense of the word into a single average-ish point in space.

This is the ceiling of this approach: embeddings are **context-independent**. They tell you what a word tends to mean *in general*, not what it means *right here*. There's also no mechanism for words the table has never seen (out-of-vocabulary tokens just fail to resolve) and no notion of word order at all — the table has no idea if "bank" was the 1st word or the 41st.

</PredictReveal>

<ConceptBox title="Where this is going">
Fixing "one vector per word, no matter the sentence" is a big part of what the rest of this track — and eventually self-attention in transformers — is about. The encoder-decoder architecture in the next module is the first step: instead of looking up a word in isolation, an RNN reads it *in sequence*, so the representation it builds can depend on everything that came before.
</ConceptBox>

---

<ConceptBox title="TL;DR">
- A word embedding is a dense vector learned so that words with similar contexts end up close together — the mechanism behind `king - man + woman ≈ queen`.
- word2vec/GloVe learn from **shared context**, not direct co-occurrence, which is why semantically related words cluster even without appearing in the same sentence.
- The core limitation: a classic embedding table returns **one fixed vector per word**, identical no matter which sentence it appears in — "bank" (river) and "bank" (money) are indistinguishable.
- No handling of out-of-vocabulary words, and no notion of word order — both motivate reading words *in sequence*, which is where the encoder-decoder architecture starts.
</ConceptBox>
