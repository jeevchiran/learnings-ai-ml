<ConceptBox title="TL;DR">
- Training error measures **memorisation**, not understanding. It's the one number that always improves with complexity, which is exactly why it can't select a model.
- $\text{Error}=\text{Bias}^2+\text{Variance}+\sigma^2$. You choose the first two; the third is a floor no model beats.
- **Bias** = systematically wrong the same way (too simple). **Variance** = answer swings with the training sample (too flexible).
- Diagnose from the *gap*: both errors high and close ⟹ underfit; training error tiny and test error far above ⟹ overfit.
- More data mainly attacks **variance**, not bias. A line stays a line no matter how many points you give it.
</ConceptBox>

## Two Models, Zero Training Error, One Winner

Fit two models to 200 houses, predicting price from square footage. Model A is a straight line. Model B is a degree-15 polynomial that threads through every training point almost exactly. By training error, B wins in a landslide — near-zero error versus A's few-thousand-dollar misses.

Ship B and it collapses. On new houses its predictions swing wildly — a house one square foot larger than a training example can get a wildly different, even negative, price, because the polynomial curves violently between memorized points. Model A, "worse" by training error, generalizes far better.

Training error measured how well each model *memorized* the data it saw, not how well it *understood* the relationship. This module builds the vocabulary — bias, variance, complexity, overfitting, underfitting — to reason about that gap. Every later technique in this track is, underneath, a way to estimate what training error cannot.

## Bias and Variance

Imagine retraining the same model on many fresh training sets from the same distribution. For a fixed test point $x$, each retraining gives a prediction $\hat{f}(x)$:

- **Bias** — how far the *average* prediction sits from the truth. High bias = systematically wrong the same way regardless of the data = too simple.
- **Variance** — how much the prediction *wiggles* between retrainings. High variance = very sensitive to the particular training sample = fits noise.

These decompose the expected squared error at a test point $x$ with $y = f(x) + \varepsilon$, noise variance $\sigma^2$:

$$\mathbb{E}\big[(y - \hat{f}(x))^2\big] = \underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] - f(x)\big)^2}_{\text{Bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat{f}(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)])^2\big]}_{\text{Variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{Noise}}$$

Expected error = how systematically wrong the model is (bias²) + how much it swings with the sample (variance) + irreducible noise no model can remove. You control the first two by choosing a model; the third is a floor.

The two trade off — drive complexity down to kill variance and bias rises; drive it up to kill bias and variance rises. Model selection finds the point minimizing their *sum*:

## Underfitting and Overfitting

- **Underfitting** = high bias: too simple to capture the structure. Symptom: training and test error both high, and close together.
- **Overfitting** = high variance: flexible enough to fit noise as signal. Symptom: low training error, much higher test error — a large *gap* is the tell.

Concretely, with true curve $y = 0.5x^2 - x + 3$ and 20 noisy points, fit polynomials of rising degree:

| Degree | Train error | Test error | Diagnosis |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (line) | ~4.8 | ~5.1 | Underfit — a line can't bend to a quadratic; wrong everywhere |
| 3 | ~0.6 | ~0.7 | Good — captures the curve, not the noise |
| 15 | ~0.02 | ~9.4 | Overfit — threads 20 noisy points, oscillates wildly between them |

The degree-15 training error is the best number on the page — and the most misleading. The huge train/test gap is the variance term made visible.

## The Complexity Knob

Every model family has a knob trading bias for variance:

- **Polynomial regression** — degree (1 rigid, 15 flexible).
- **Ridge/Lasso** — regularization $\lambda$ (`alpha`): large shrinks coefficients (higher bias), small fits freely (higher variance).
- **Decision trees** — max depth: shallow is coarse/high-bias, unbounded memorizes noise.
- **k-NN** — $k$: $k=1$ follows the single nearest point (high variance); large $k$ averages many (higher bias).

```python
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeRegressor
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsRegressor
from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge
from sklearn.preprocessing import PolynomialFeatures
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

# One "complexity knob" idea, four model families
shallow_tree = DecisionTreeRegressor(max_depth=2)     # high bias, low variance
deep_tree    = DecisionTreeRegressor(max_depth=None)  # low bias, high variance
knn_smooth   = KNeighborsRegressor(n_neighbors=25)    # high bias, low variance
knn_jagged   = KNeighborsRegressor(n_neighbors=1)     # low bias, high variance
ridge_strong = Ridge(alpha=100.0)                     # high bias, low variance
ridge_weak   = Ridge(alpha=0.001)                     # low bias, high variance
```

## Why Training Error Cannot Select a Model

Training error is a *biased, optimistic* estimate of generalization, and the bias worsens with flexibility. The extreme case: a lookup table that memorizes every training pair achieves exactly zero training error and generalizes to nothing. Judged by training error, it wins every comparison.

This is the general pattern — more flexible models can always fit the training set at least as well, so training error falls monotonically with complexity whether the added flexibility captures signal or noise. It literally cannot tell the two apart. You need performance on data the model didn't see during fitting: **held-out data**, the subject of the next module.

<ConceptBox title="Two different levers — don't confuse them">
- **More training data** shrinks variance (each retraining sees a fuller sample) but does nothing for bias — a line fit to a curve stays just as wrong with 10 million points, only more consistently wrong.
- **A more complex model** shrinks bias but raises variance.

So: underfitting (high bias) → more data won't help; you need a richer model or better features. Overfitting (high variance) → more data usually helps, alongside reducing complexity.
</ConceptBox>

## Practice

#### Problem 1: Diagnosing from the Error Gap
Model 1: 96% train, 95% test. Model 2: 99.8% train, 81% test. Diagnose each.

**Solution:** Model 1 has a 1-point gap with both numbers high — a well-fit model, no obvious under/overfitting. Model 2 has an 18.8-point gap with near-perfect training — the textbook overfitting signature: it nearly memorized the training set and that didn't transfer. Reduce complexity (fewer features, stronger regularization, shallower tree, larger $k$) or collect more data.

#### Problem 2: Picking the Complexity Knob
A $k=1$ k-NN badly overfits noisy data. What change to $k$, and why per the decomposition?

**Solution:** Increase $k$. At $k=1$ each prediction depends on the single nearest point — very low bias, very high variance (one noisy neighbour flips it). Larger $k$ averages the vote over more neighbours, cutting variance at the cost of slightly higher bias. Since the error was variance-dominated, this is a net win until $k$ grows large enough for bias to dominate.
