## Why average over several splits

The holdout method (Module 2) splits your data once into train/validation/test — and as you saw, that single validation score has high variance: get a lucky split and your model looks great, get an unlucky one and it looks mediocre, even though nothing about the model changed. Cross-validation fixes this by not relying on a single split at all.

The fix is conceptually simple: instead of validating on one held-out chunk, validate on several different chunks and average the scores. Each chunk gives a noisy estimate of true generalization performance; averaging several noisy estimates gives a much more stable one. This is the same logic as polling more people to shrink the margin of error — more (independent-ish) samples, less noise in the estimate.

## K-Fold CV: the mechanics

**K-Fold cross-validation** splits the full training data into $K$ equally-sized folds. Then, for each fold $k = 1, \ldots, K$:

1. Hold out fold $k$ as the validation set.
2. Train the model on the remaining $K-1$ folds.
3. Score the trained model on fold $k$.

After all $K$ rounds, every data point has been used for validation exactly once and for training $K-1$ times. The final CV estimate is the mean of the $K$ scores:

$$\widehat{\text{CV}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K \text{score}_k$$

This says: average the K validation scores, one per fold, to get a single summary number for "how good is this model configuration."

```python
from sklearn.model_selection import KFold, cross_val_score
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier

kf = KFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=42)
model = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=200, random_state=42)

scores = cross_val_score(model, X_train, y_train, cv=kf, scoring='accuracy')
print(scores)          # array of 5 fold scores
print(scores.mean(), scores.std())   # CV estimate ± spread
```

Reporting both the mean *and* the standard deviation across folds matters — two models with the same mean CV score but very different fold-to-fold spread are not equally trustworthy.

### Choosing K: a bias-variance tradeoff on the estimate itself

$K$ is itself a hyperparameter of the evaluation procedure, and it has its own bias-variance tradeoff:

| K | Training fold size | Bias of CV estimate | Variance of CV estimate | Compute cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Small (e.g. 3) | Smaller (fewer examples to train on each round) | Higher — each model is trained on less data, so it under-performs the "real" full-data model | Lower — folds are large and stable, so score-to-score is less jumpy | Cheap ($K=3$ fits) |
| Large (e.g. 10 or $N$) | Larger, closer to full dataset | Lower — training sets nearly match full-data training | Higher — small validation folds mean each score is noisier | Expensive ($K$ fits, or $N$ fits) |

$K=5$ or $K=10$ is the standard default because it sits in a sweet spot: training folds are large enough that the bias from "training on less data" is small, while validation folds are still large enough that individual fold scores aren't too noisy — all at a reasonable compute cost ($K$ model fits total).

## Stratified K-Fold: fixing class imbalance

Plain `KFold` splits indices without looking at the labels. On an imbalanced dataset (say 95% negative, 5% positive), a random fold can easily end up with a wildly different positive rate than the overall dataset — or in the worst case, a fold with *zero* positive examples, making that fold's score meaningless.

**Stratified K-Fold** fixes this by constructing folds so each one preserves (approximately) the overall class proportions.

```python
from sklearn.model_selection import StratifiedKFold, cross_val_score

skf = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=42)
scores = cross_val_score(model, X_train, y_train, cv=skf, scoring='f1')
```

For classification tasks, `StratifiedKFold` should be your default over plain `KFold` — there's essentially no downside, and it removes a real source of noise.

## Leave-One-Out (LOO)

**Leave-One-Out** is the extreme case: $K = N$. Each fold holds out exactly one example; the model trains on the other $N-1$ and is scored on that single point. This means fitting $N$ separate models.

```python
from sklearn.model_selection import LeaveOneOut, cross_val_score

loo = LeaveOneOut()
scores = cross_val_score(model, X_train, y_train, cv=loo, scoring='accuracy')
```

LOO is used almost exclusively on **very small datasets** (dozens to low hundreds of rows), where you can't afford to hold out a large validation fold and every training example matters.

<ConceptBox title="LOO's subtle bias-variance quirk">
LOO looks maximally thorough — $K=N$ means the least biased possible estimate, since each model trains on almost the entire dataset. That part is true: LOO is nearly unbiased.

But the *variance* of the LOO estimate is not automatically low, and can actually be **higher** than $K=10$ CV. Why? The $N$ training sets in LOO overlap almost completely — each one differs from the others by only a single swapped example. That means the $N$ resulting models are highly correlated with each other, and so are their errors. Averaging $N$ *highly correlated* estimates doesn't reduce variance nearly as much as averaging $K$ *more independent* ones does — you're not really getting $N$ independent looks at performance, you're getting $N$ near-duplicates. This is why $K=10$ is often preferred over LOO even when LOO is computationally feasible: fewer, more different, less-correlated folds can give an estimate with equal or lower total error.
</ConceptBox>

## Group and time-series CV: when shuffling leaks information

Plain (or stratified) K-Fold assumes each row is an independent sample. Two common situations break that assumption:

**Grouped data.** If your dataset has multiple rows per patient, per user, or per device, a random split can put some of a patient's rows in the training fold and others in the validation fold. The model can then partly "recognize" that patient from training and get an inflated validation score that has nothing to do with generalizing to *new* patients. `GroupKFold` keeps all rows belonging to the same group entirely in one fold or the other:

```python
from sklearn.model_selection import GroupKFold, cross_val_score

gkf = GroupKFold(n_splits=5)
scores = cross_val_score(model, X_train, y_train, cv=gkf, groups=patient_ids)
```

**Time-ordered data.** If rows are ordered in time (stock prices, sensor logs, user sessions over months), a random K-Fold split will happily put future rows in the training set and past rows in the validation set. The model then gets to "see the future" during training — a leak that produces an optimistic score you'll never achieve in production, where you only ever have the past to predict the future.

`TimeSeriesSplit` avoids this with an **expanding window**: fold 1 trains on an early chunk and validates on the chunk right after it; fold 2 trains on everything up to that point (original chunk *plus* the previous validation chunk) and validates on the next chunk after that; and so on. Training data only ever grows forward in time, and validation data is always strictly *after* the training data it's paired with.

```python
from sklearn.model_selection import TimeSeriesSplit, cross_val_score

tscv = TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=5)
scores = cross_val_score(model, X_train, y_train, cv=tscv, scoring='neg_mean_squared_error')
```

```
Fold 1: train [1........]        val [2]
Fold 2: train [1........2]       val [3]
Fold 3: train [1........2..3]    val [4]
Fold 4: train [1........2..3.4]  val [5]
```

<ConceptBox title="Common confusion: does K-Fold CV give you K models?">
It's tempting to think K-Fold CV trains K models and you keep all of them, maybe averaging their predictions at deployment time (that's actually a different technique — ensembling). That's **not** what CV is for.

K-Fold CV gives you $K$ performance **estimates** of one *model-training procedure* — e.g. "a random forest with `max_depth=8` trained on this kind of data." Those K scores exist purely to answer the question "how good is this configuration, honestly?" Once you've picked the best configuration using CV, the model you actually deploy is typically **retrained once from scratch on the full training dataset**, using the hyperparameters that CV validated. The K fold-models built during CV are discarded — they were tools for measurement, not the final product.
</ConceptBox>

## Practice

#### Problem 1: Picking a CV strategy

You have 50,000 rows of hospital vitals data, with roughly 10 rows per patient (multiple readings per patient over their stay), and the task is to predict a rare complication that occurs in about 2% of patients. Which CV strategy should you use, and why?

**Solution:** You need to combine two constraints at once. The label is rare (2%), so plain `KFold` risks folds with very few or zero positive examples — you want stratification. But rows aren't independent: 10 rows share a patient, so a random or stratified split could leak a patient's data across train and validation. The correct approach is a grouped, stratified split — e.g. `StratifiedGroupKFold` — splitting by `patient_id` as the group while still balancing the complication rate across folds. Plain `StratifiedKFold` alone would leak patient identity; plain `GroupKFold` alone risks folds with too few positive patients.

#### Problem 2: Interpreting fold scores

A 10-fold CV run gives scores `[0.91, 0.60, 0.89, 0.92, 0.88, 0.90, 0.87, 0.91, 0.89, 0.90]`. Mean is 0.867. What should you investigate before trusting this number?

**Solution:** One fold (0.60) is a dramatic outlier compared to the other nine, which cluster tightly around 0.87–0.92. A single mean hides this. Before trusting 0.867 as "the" CV score, check the standard deviation (it will be inflated by this one fold) and inspect what's different about that fold — a data quality issue, a rare-but-important subgroup concentrated there, or (if the split wasn't stratified/grouped) a leak-related fluke. Report the spread alongside the mean, not just the mean.
