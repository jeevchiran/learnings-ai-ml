## Why Average Over Several Splits

The holdout method (Module 2) splits once, and that single validation score has high variance — a lucky split flatters the model, an unlucky one doesn't, with no change to the model itself. Cross-validation stops relying on a single split.

The idea: validate on several different chunks and average. Each chunk is a noisy estimate of true generalization; averaging several gives a far more stable one — the same logic as polling more people to shrink the margin of error.

## K-Fold CV: Mechanics

**K-Fold** splits the training data into $K$ equal folds. For each fold $k$:

1. Hold out fold $k$ as validation.
2. Train on the other $K-1$ folds.
3. Score on fold $k$.

Every point is validated once and trained on $K-1$ times. The estimate is the mean:

$$\widehat{\text{CV}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K \text{score}_k$$

```python
from sklearn.model_selection import KFold, cross_val_score
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier

kf = KFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=42)
model = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=200, random_state=42)

scores = cross_val_score(model, X_train, y_train, cv=kf, scoring='accuracy')
print(scores)                        # 5 fold scores
print(scores.mean(), scores.std())   # CV estimate ± spread
```

Report the **standard deviation** too — two models with equal mean but different fold-to-fold spread aren't equally trustworthy.

### Choosing K

$K$ has its own bias-variance tradeoff on the *estimate*:

| K | Training folds | Bias | Variance | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Small (3) | Smaller | Higher (each model trains on less data) | Lower (large, stable folds) | Cheap |
| Large (10 or $N$) | Near-full | Lower | Higher (small, noisy folds) | Expensive |

$K=5$ or $K=10$ is the standard sweet spot: training folds large enough that bias is small, validation folds large enough that scores aren't too noisy, at reasonable cost.

## Stratified K-Fold

Plain `KFold` ignores labels. On imbalanced data (95/5), a random fold can have a wildly different positive rate — or zero positives, making its score meaningless. **Stratified K-Fold** builds folds that preserve class proportions.

```python
from sklearn.model_selection import StratifiedKFold, cross_val_score

skf = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=42)
scores = cross_val_score(model, X_train, y_train, cv=skf, scoring='f1')
```

For classification, make `StratifiedKFold` the default — no downside, removes a real source of noise.

## Leave-One-Out (LOO)

The extreme $K = N$: each fold holds out one example, trains on $N-1$, scores on that point — $N$ model fits. Used almost only on **very small datasets** where you can't spare a large validation fold.

```python
from sklearn.model_selection import LeaveOneOut, cross_val_score

loo = LeaveOneOut()
scores = cross_val_score(model, X_train, y_train, cv=loo, scoring='accuracy')
```

<ConceptBox title="LOO's variance quirk">
LOO looks maximally thorough — $K=N$, nearly unbiased since each model trains on almost all the data. True. But its *variance* can be **higher** than $K=10$: the $N$ training sets differ by only one swapped example, so the $N$ models — and their errors — are highly correlated. Averaging $N$ near-duplicates reduces variance far less than averaging $K$ more independent folds. Hence $K=10$ is often preferred even when LOO is affordable.
</ConceptBox>

## Group and Time-Series CV

Plain K-Fold assumes independent rows. Two situations break that:

**Grouped data.** Multiple rows per patient/user/device — a random split puts some of a patient's rows in train and others in validation, so the model "recognizes" that patient and inflates its score. `GroupKFold` keeps each group wholly in one fold.

```python
from sklearn.model_selection import GroupKFold, cross_val_score

gkf = GroupKFold(n_splits=5)
scores = cross_val_score(model, X_train, y_train, cv=gkf, groups=patient_ids)
```

**Time-ordered data.** A random split puts future rows in training and past rows in validation — the model "sees the future," an optimistic score you'll never hit in production. `TimeSeriesSplit` uses an **expanding window**: training only ever grows forward, validation is always strictly after it.

```python
from sklearn.model_selection import TimeSeriesSplit, cross_val_score

tscv = TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=5)
scores = cross_val_score(model, X_train, y_train, cv=tscv, scoring='neg_mean_squared_error')
```

<ConceptBox title="Does K-Fold CV give you K models?">
No. K-Fold gives $K$ performance **estimates** of one *training procedure* (e.g. "a random forest with `max_depth=8`"). Those scores answer "how good is this configuration, honestly?" Once you pick the best config, you **retrain once on the full training set** with those hyperparameters. The K fold-models are discarded — measurement tools, not the product. (Keeping and averaging them is a different technique, ensembling.)
</ConceptBox>

## Practice

#### Problem 1: Picking a CV Strategy
50,000 rows, ~10 rows per patient, predicting a 2% complication. Which strategy?

**Solution:** Two constraints. The rare label needs stratification (plain `KFold` risks folds with zero positives); the 10-rows-per-patient structure needs grouping (a random split leaks patient identity across train/validation). Combine them: `StratifiedGroupKFold`, grouping by `patient_id` while balancing the complication rate. Stratified alone leaks patients; grouped alone risks too few positive patients per fold.

#### Problem 2: Interpreting Fold Scores
10-fold scores `[0.91, 0.60, 0.89, 0.92, 0.88, 0.90, 0.87, 0.91, 0.89, 0.90]`, mean 0.867. What to check before trusting it?

**Solution:** One fold (0.60) is a dramatic outlier; the mean hides it. Check the standard deviation (inflated by this fold) and inspect that fold — a data-quality issue, a rare subgroup concentrated there, or a leak if the split wasn't stratified/grouped. Report the spread alongside the mean.
