<ConceptBox title="TL;DR">
- A recommender **ranks a huge catalog into a few slots**. It is not "predict a rating" — that framing died with the Netflix Prize.
- Every production system is a **funnel**: retrieval (millions → hundreds, must be fast, scored on *recall*) then ranking (hundreds → ten, may be expensive, scored on *precision*).
- The catalog is enormous, the feedback is implicit, and **the model's own output becomes tomorrow's training data**. Those three facts cause every hard problem in the field.
- Pick the problem type from the **surface** you are building for. "Similar items" on a product page and "for you" on the homepage are different problems wearing the same clothes.
</ConceptBox>

## The store we will build

Meet **NovaCart** — an online store with a catalog, shoppers who browse far more than they buy, and ten slots on the homepage. Every module in this track uses the same tiny NovaCart dataset: 6 shoppers, 9 products, 30 days of events. Small enough to check every number by hand, big enough that popularity, recency and collaborative filtering all give different answers.

The business question is always the same: **which ten products, for this shopper, right now?**

## Why this is not just classification

You could frame it as "predict P(purchase | user, item)" and train a classifier. People do. But three things make it its own field:

| Fact | Consequence |
|---|---|
| The candidate set is the whole catalog | Scoring 1M items per request in 50 ms is impossible. You need a cheap first stage. |
| Feedback is **implicit** and one-sided | A click is weak evidence of interest. A non-click is *not* evidence of dislike — they may never have seen it. |
| Your output becomes your next training set | Recommend only popular items and you generate data proving popular items are what people want. |

That last one has a name — the **feedback loop** — and it is the reason offline metrics and online results diverge so often.

## The funnel

Nobody scores a million products with a good model. They score a million with a cheap model, then a few hundred with an expensive one.

<ConceptBox title="The asymmetry that matters">
Retrieval and ranking are graded on **different metrics because they can make different mistakes**.

Retrieval's only failure is *dropping* a good item — once it is gone, no downstream stage can recover it. So retrieval is tuned for **recall**: get the good stuff into the candidate set, junk is tolerable.

Ranking's failure is *ordering* — the good item is present but sits at position 40. So ranking is tuned for **precision at the top**: NDCG@10, not recall@500.

Optimising retrieval for precision is the classic beginner mistake. It makes the candidate set look great and quietly caps how good your ranker can ever be.
</ConceptBox>

## Problem types

"Recommender system" is a family, not one task. Choose by what the surface has to do:

| Type | The question | NovaCart surface | Typical model |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rating prediction** | How many stars would they give? | (rare in e-commerce) | Explicit MF |
| **Top-N retrieval** | Which N items from the whole catalog? | Homepage "for you" | Two-tower, ALS, item-kNN |
| **Item-to-item** | Given *this* item, what else? | "Similar products" on a product page | Co-visitation, content embeddings |
| **Sequential / next-item** | What comes next in this session? | "Continue where you left off" | GRU4Rec, SASRec, transformers |
| **Basket completion** | What goes *with* what is already in the cart? | "Frequently bought together" | Association rules, co-purchase |
| **Re-ranking** | Given a candidate list, order it | Search results, category pages | Gradient-boosted ranker (LTR) |
| **Cold-start** | Brand new item or shopper | New-arrivals row | Content / multi-modal |

The mistakes here are expensive and common. Serving a "similar items" model on the homepage produces a page of nine variants of the last thing viewed. Serving a "for you" model on a product page ignores the strongest signal available — the product being looked at right now.

## Position bias, the tax on every log

Items at the top get clicked more **because they are at the top**, not only because they are better. Your training log records the outcome of your own layout as if it were shopper preference.

Two consequences you will meet repeatedly:

- A "non-click" on position 30 is nearly meaningless — the shopper probably never scrolled that far. Treating it as a negative teaches the model your old ranking was correct.
- Offline improvement on logged data can be pure **position-bias exploitation**. This is why the last word always belongs to an online A/B test.
