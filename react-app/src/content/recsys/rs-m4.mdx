<ConceptBox title="TL;DR">
- Ranking metrics differ in **what they are indifferent to**. Pick the one whose blind spot you can live with.
- **Precision@k** — of the slots you spent, what fraction paid off. **Recall@k** — of what they wanted, what fraction you found.
- **MRR** cares only about the *first* hit. **MAP** averages precision at every hit. **NDCG** is the only common one that discounts by *position* and normalises by the best possible order.
- Always report a **beyond-accuracy** metric too — coverage or intra-list diversity — or you will ship a model that recommends the same five products to everyone and scores well doing it.
</ConceptBox>

## The formulas

Let $R$ be the set of relevant items and $\text{rel}(i)=1$ if the item at rank $i$ is in $R$.

<DerivationSteps>
<DerivationStep number={1} title="Precision@k and Recall@k">
$$P@k = \frac{|\text{top-}k \cap R|}{k}, \qquad R@k = \frac{|\text{top-}k \cap R|}{|R|}$$
Same numerator, different denominator. Precision divides by what you *spent*; recall divides by what was *available*. Precision@k generally falls as k grows, recall@k can only rise.
</DerivationStep>

<DerivationStep number={2} title="MRR — reciprocal rank of the first hit">
$$MRR = \frac{1}{|U|}\sum_{u} \frac{1}{\text{rank of }u\text{'s first relevant item}}$$
Rank 1 scores 1.0, rank 2 scores 0.5, rank 4 scores 0.25. **Everything after the first hit is invisible to MRR.** Correct for "did they find the one thing they came for", wrong for a browsing surface.
</DerivationStep>

<DerivationStep number={3} title="MAP — precision averaged at the hit positions">
$$AP@k = \frac{1}{\min(k,|R|)}\sum_{i=1}^{k} P@i \cdot \text{rel}(i)$$
Compute precision at every position where you got a hit, average those. MAP is the mean of $AP$ over users. It rewards putting *all* the relevant items early, not just one.
</DerivationStep>

<DerivationStep number={4} title="NDCG — position-discounted gain, normalised">
$$DCG@k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{\text{rel}(i)}{\log_2(i+1)}, \qquad NDCG@k = \frac{DCG@k}{IDCG@k}$$
The $\log_2(i+1)$ discount says a hit at rank 1 is worth 1.00, rank 2 is worth 0.63, rank 3 is worth 0.50. $IDCG@k$ is the DCG of the perfect ordering (all relevant items stacked at the top), so NDCG lands in $[0,1]$ and is comparable across users with different numbers of relevant items.
</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

## Worked example — the same slate, four answers

Slate: `Steel Bottle, Earbuds, Phone Case, USB-C Hub, Laptop Stand, Yoga Mat, Keyboard, Grinder`
Relevant: `Earbuds (rank 2), USB-C Hub (rank 4), Yoga Mat (rank 6)`

At $k=3$ there is exactly one hit, at rank 2:

$$P@3 = \tfrac{1}{3} = 0.333, \qquad R@3 = \tfrac{1}{3} = 0.333, \qquad RR = \tfrac{1}{2} = 0.500$$

$$AP@3 = \frac{P@2 \cdot 1}{\min(3,3)} = \frac{0.5}{3} = 0.167$$

$$DCG@3 = \frac{1}{\log_2 3} = 0.631, \quad IDCG@3 = 1 + \tfrac{1}{\log_2 3} + \tfrac{1}{\log_2 4} = 2.131$$

$$NDCG@3 = \frac{0.631}{2.131} = 0.296$$

Now move to $k=5$ — a second hit enters at rank 4:

$$P@5 = \tfrac{2}{5} = 0.400 \;(\text{up}), \qquad R@5 = \tfrac{2}{3} = 0.667 \;(\text{up}), \qquad RR = 0.500 \;(\text{unchanged})$$

Precision went **up** while k grew, which is the opposite of the usual story — because the extra slot happened to contain a hit. Recall rose. MRR did not move at all: it stopped paying attention after rank 2. Three metrics, three different verdicts on the same change.

<ConceptBox title="Common confusion — NDCG@k with a single relevant item">
Most e-commerce evaluations hold out **one** next purchase per shopper. With $|R|=1$, $IDCG@k = 1$ always, so

$$NDCG@k = \frac{1}{\log_2(\text{rank}+1)} \quad\text{if the item is in the top }k,\ \text{else } 0$$

which is just a smoothed version of MRR. In that setup NDCG and MRR rank your models almost identically, and reporting both looks like two pieces of evidence when it is one. NDCG earns its keep when there are several relevant items, or when relevance is graded (purchase = 3, cart = 2, view = 1) rather than binary.
</ConceptBox>

## Accuracy is not enough

A model that recommends the five best-selling products to every shopper can post a respectable HitRate. It is also useless — it sells nothing that would not have sold anyway, and it starves the catalog.

| Metric | Question | Failure it catches |
|---|---|---|
| **Catalog coverage** | What fraction of products appear in *anyone's* top-k? | The model shows everyone the same slate |
| **Intra-list diversity** | How dissimilar are the items within one slate? | Nine variants of the same product |
| **Novelty** | How popular, on average, are the items shown? | The model only ever recommends the head |
| **Serendipity** | Are hits ones the shopper would *not* have found alone? | The model recommends what they already bought |

Report at least one of these alongside NDCG. In the next module's baseline table you will see the popularity model post a real HitRate with **33% coverage** — three products out of nine ever shown. The accuracy number alone would not have told you.
