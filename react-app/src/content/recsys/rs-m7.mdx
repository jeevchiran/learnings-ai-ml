<ConceptBox title="TL;DR">
- Collaborative filtering uses **only the interaction matrix** — no product titles, no shopper demographics. "People like you liked this."
- **Item-item CF** ("shoppers who viewed this also viewed…") beats user-user CF in practice: item neighbourhoods are stabler than user tastes, and the similarity matrix can be precomputed offline.
- Similarity is cosine over columns of the matrix. Two products are similar if the **same shoppers** touched both.
- Two structural failures: **cold-start** (a new product has an empty column, so it is similar to nothing) and **popularity bias** (popular items are similar to everything).
</ConceptBox>

## The idea in one sentence

Aarav and Bhavna both looked at the Earbuds, the Keyboard and the USB-C Hub. Bhavna also looked at the Steel Bottle. **Show Aarav the Steel Bottle.**

No one told the system what a keyboard *is*. The only input is the matrix of who touched what.

## Two directions through the same matrix

<CFSimilarityWidget />

**User-user CF**: find shoppers similar to you, recommend what they liked.
**Item-item CF**: find items similar to the ones you liked, recommend those.

They read the same matrix along different axes, and in production item-item wins almost every time:

| | User-user | Item-item |
|---|---|---|
| Neighbourhood stability | Tastes shift weekly | An item's audience is stable for months |
| Precompute | Recompute as users act | Item×item matrix precomputes nightly |
| Scale | Millions of users | Usually far fewer items |
| New user | Useless until they act | Works from their **first** click |
| Explainability | "Similar shoppers liked it" | "Because you viewed the Keyboard" — shows the actual reason |

That last row matters more than it looks. "Because you viewed X" is a real explanation the shopper can verify, which is why item-item CF has survived a decade of deep-learning replacements.

## The math

<DerivationSteps>
<DerivationStep number={1} title="Similarity — cosine between two item columns">
$$\text{sim}(i,j) = \frac{\mathbf{r}_i \cdot \mathbf{r}_j}{\lVert \mathbf{r}_i \rVert \, \lVert \mathbf{r}_j \rVert}$$
where $\mathbf{r}_i$ is item $i$'s column: one entry per shopper. Cosine ignores magnitude, so an item with many interactions is not automatically similar to everything — only to items touched by the *same* shoppers.
</DerivationStep>

<DerivationStep number={2} title="Score — weighted vote from the shopper's own history">
$$\text{score}(u,i) = \sum_{j \in \text{history}(u)} \text{sim}(i,j) \cdot r_{uj}$$
Every item the shopper already touched casts a vote for its neighbours, weighted by how similar they are and how strongly the shopper engaged. Take the top-k scores, filter out what they have already seen, serve.
</DerivationStep>

<DerivationStep number={3} title="Optional — normalise and truncate">
$$\text{score}(u,i) = \frac{\sum_{j \in N_k(i)\,\cap\,\text{history}(u)} \text{sim}(i,j)\, r_{uj}}{\sum_{j \in N_k(i)\,\cap\,\text{history}(u)} |\text{sim}(i,j)|}$$
Restricting to item $i$'s top-$k$ neighbours $N_k(i)$ cuts noise from thousands of near-zero similarities and makes the whole thing sparse enough to serve. $k$ between 20 and 100 is typical.
</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

## Worked example

Binary NovaCart training matrix. Earbuds (P1) were touched by Aarav, Bhavna, Eli. The Keyboard (P4) was touched by Aarav, Bhavna.

$$\text{sim}(P1,P4) = \frac{|\{U1,U2\}|}{\sqrt{3 \cdot 2}} = \frac{2}{2.449} = 0.816$$

Yoga Mat (P7) was touched by Diya and Farida; the Steel Bottle (P8) by Bhavna, Chen, Diya, Eli, Farida:

$$\text{sim}(P7,P8) = \frac{|\{U4,U6\}|}{\sqrt{2 \cdot 5}} = \frac{2}{3.162} = 0.632$$

The Yoga Mat / Steel Bottle pair is the one that *reads* right — two fitness products. The Earbuds / Keyboard pair scores higher and is mostly an artefact of two shoppers with broad histories. That is not a flaw in the formula; it is what 34 events buys you. **CF similarity needs support**, and the standard defence is a minimum co-occurrence threshold: discard any pair sharing fewer than, say, 20 shoppers before ranking.

<ConceptBox title="Common confusion — cosine already handles popularity, mostly">
It is often said that popular items are similar to everything. Cosine's denominator resists this: the Steel Bottle appears in 5 of 6 histories, but dividing by $\lVert \mathbf{r}_{P8} \rVert = \sqrt{5}$ pulls its similarities back down.

What cosine does **not** fix is the *score* step. A popular item is in many shoppers' neighbourhoods, so it accumulates votes from many directions and rises in almost everyone's ranking. The fix lives there, not in the similarity: divide the final score by $\text{pop}(i)^\beta$ with $\beta$ around 0.5, or sample negatives by popularity (module 11).
</ConceptBox>

## Where CF structurally fails

**Cold-start.** The ANC Headphones (P9) have zero interactions, so their column is all zeros, so every similarity is exactly 0. CF cannot rank them — not badly, but *at all*. Since they cannot be recommended, they cannot earn interactions, so they stay cold. Module 12 breaks this loop with content.

**Sparsity.** Real e-commerce matrices are ~99.9% empty. Most item pairs share zero shoppers, so most similarities are 0 and the useful signal is concentrated in the head of the catalog.

**No features.** Price, brand, "in stock", "on sale", time of day — CF sees none of it. A CF model will happily recommend an out-of-stock product to someone who bought it yesterday.

Each of these has an answer in the following modules: latent factors for sparsity (module 8), features and a ranker for the third (modules 9–10), and content embeddings for cold-start (module 12).
