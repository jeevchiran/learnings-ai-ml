<ConceptBox title="TL;DR">
- A recommender is deployed **forward in time**. Evaluate it that way: one global time cut-off, train on the past, test on the future.
- **Random splits leak.** They let the model train on day 30 and be tested on day 2 — an information advantage it will never have in production.
- **Leave-one-out per user** is popular in papers and still leaks: each user's cut-off is a different wall-clock moment, so one shopper's test event is inside another shopper's training window.
- Leakage always shows up the same way: offline metrics improve, the A/B test does not move.
</ConceptBox>

## The only question a split has to answer

*Would this model, trained only on what was knowable at time T, have made good recommendations after T?*

Every split strategy is an approximation to that question. Some approximate it well.

## The three splits, ranked

| Split | Leaks? | Use it when |
|---|---|---|
| **Global temporal cut-off** | No | Always, as your headline number |
| **Leave-one-out per user** | Yes, mildly | Comparing against published papers that used it |
| **Random 80/20** | Badly | Never, for interaction data |

<ConceptBox title="Why random splitting is worse than it looks">
Randomly holding out 20% of NovaCart's events puts Farida's day-30 purchase in training and her day-2 view in test. Three separate things go wrong:

1. **Future leaks into the past.** The model knows what happens later, then is graded on earlier events. That advantage does not exist at serving time.
2. **Popularity leaks.** An item's total popularity is computed over the full window, including the test period. Even a pure popularity baseline gets an unearned boost.
3. **Session leaks.** Events from the same session land on both sides of the split. Predicting the 4th click of a session when you have already seen the 5th is not a prediction.

The result is uniformly optimistic and *differentially* optimistic — it flatters the models best at memorising, which are usually not the ones you want to ship.
</ConceptBox>

## Leave-one-out, and why papers use it anyway

Hold out each shopper's most recent event. Every shopper contributes exactly one test case, so per-user metrics are balanced and no shopper is excluded for having little history.

The cost: shoppers' "last events" happen at different times. Chen's last event is day 28; Diya's is day 25. Chen's test event sits inside the training window of everyone whose history extends past day 28, so the model has seen the future relative to Diya. Item popularity is again computed with partial test knowledge.

Toggle the widget to leave-one-out and count the red dots. It is a mild leak, not a catastrophic one — which is exactly why it survives: results are comparable across papers, and the bias is roughly constant across models. **Use it to compare with published numbers; use the temporal split to decide what to ship.**

## The other leaks

Fixing the split is necessary, not sufficient. Four more, in the order teams usually hit them:

- **Feature leakage.** A feature computed over the whole log — "total purchases by this shopper" — contains the test period. This is module 10's entire subject, and it is the most common of all.
- **Preprocessing leakage.** Fitting a scaler, an encoder, or an item-embedding table on all data before splitting. The statistics carry test information.
- **Tuning leakage.** Choosing $k$, $\alpha$ and $\lambda$ by their score on the test set. Do that fifty times and your test set is a training set. Use a **validation window** carved out before the test window: train on days 1–20, tune on 21–24, report on 25–30.
- **Cold-entity leakage.** Users or items that appear *only* in test. They are not leakage themselves, but silently dropping them is: real traffic contains new shoppers, so excluding them reports a number for a world you do not serve.

<ConceptBox title="Common confusion — 'my temporal split still gives a suspiciously high score'">
Check three things before believing it.

**Is the test window long enough?** A one-day test window on a store with weekly seasonality measures the day of the week, not the model.

**Did popularity get recomputed?** If your item-popularity table was built once over the full log, every model consuming it is leaking, no matter how clean the event split is.

**Are you filtering seen items?** Recommending what a shopper already viewed scores extremely well offline, because e-commerce shoppers return to finish purchases. That is a real effect, not a bug — but it means "recently viewed" is your true baseline, and a model that only beats *random* has beaten nothing.
</ConceptBox>
