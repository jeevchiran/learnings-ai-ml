## The Black-Box Problem

A model can be extremely accurate and still be useless. A bank's neural network denies a loan; the applicant asks *why*; the bank shrugs. A hospital model flags a scan as malignant; the radiologist needs to know *what in the image* triggered it before acting. Accuracy alone answers "what" — it never answers "why."

**Explainable AI (XAI)** is the set of methods that make a model's decisions understandable to a human — turning "the model said 0.92" into "the model said 0.92 *because* income was high and the loan amount was small."

<ConceptBox title="Two words people mix up">
**Interpretability** — how well a human can grasp the model's mechanism on its own (a 3-line decision tree is interpretable). **Explainability** — techniques applied *after the fact* to shine a light into a model that isn't interpretable by itself (SHAP on a deep net). XAI mostly lives in the second bucket.
</ConceptBox>

## Complexity vs. Interpretability

The uncomfortable pattern: the models that win on hard problems are the hardest to read. A linear model hands you its reasoning in a row of coefficients. A 100-million-parameter network hands you nothing legible. Explore the trade-off:

## Why It's Actually Useful

Explanations are not a nicety. They earn their keep in concrete ways:

| Need | What an explanation buys you |
|---|---|
| **Trust** | A doctor acts on a prediction only after seeing it points at the right region of the scan. |
| **Debugging** | You discover the model keys on a hospital's watermark, not the tumour — a leak you'd never catch from accuracy. |
| **Fairness / compliance** | GDPR and lending law require a *reason* for automated decisions, not just a score. |
| **Discovery** | The explanation surfaces a feature interaction nobody knew mattered. |

<ConceptBox title="The Clever Hans trap">
A famous vision model classified horses correctly — by reading a copyright tag present only on horse photos. Test accuracy was excellent; the model had learned nothing about horses. Only an explanation exposed it. High accuracy on a held-out set does **not** prove the model is right for the right reasons.
</ConceptBox>

## Practice

#### Problem 1: Accuracy isn't enough
A fraud model scores 99.4% accuracy on held-out data. Give one reason you might still refuse to deploy it.

**Solution:** Accuracy can hide *why* it's right. It might exploit a leak (e.g. a timestamp that only exists for labelled fraud), fail on a subgroup, or be legally undeployable without a stated reason per decision. An explanation reveals whether the 99.4% comes from genuine signal or an artefact.

#### Problem 2: Interpretability vs. explainability
Classify each: (a) reading a logistic regression's weights, (b) running SHAP on a random forest.

**Solution:** (a) **Interpretability** — the model is transparent by construction; you read its own mechanism. (b) **Explainability** — the forest is opaque, so you apply an external post-hoc technique to explain it.
