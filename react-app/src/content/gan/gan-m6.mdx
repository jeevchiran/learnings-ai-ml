## A divergence that goes flat on disjoint supports

<PredictReveal
  prompt="Early in training — and often for a long time after — the real data and the generator's output occupy almost non-overlapping regions of image space. In that regime, what happens to the gradient the original GAN loss provides?"
  options={["It stays informative, since the loss is designed for exactly this case", "It can vanish or become erratic, because the underlying divergence is locally flat or undefined when the two distributions barely overlap"]}
  correct={1}
>

The original GAN objective corresponds to minimizing the Jensen-Shannon divergence between $p_{data}$ and $p_g$. When the two distributions have little or no overlapping support — the normal state of affairs for high-dimensional image data early in training — JS divergence is either flat or saturates, giving little useful gradient regardless of which loss variant (Module 2's or Module 3's) is used. The problem is not the loss rewrite; it is the divergence underneath it.

</PredictReveal>

## Wasserstein distance and the Lipschitz constraint

<PredictReveal
  prompt="The Wasserstein (Earth Mover's) distance measures the minimum 'cost' to move probability mass from one distribution to match the other. Unlike JS divergence, does it stay meaningful and provide gradient even when the two distributions have disjoint support?"
  options={["No — it also goes flat on disjoint supports, for the same underlying reason", "Yes — it varies smoothly with how far apart the distributions are, even with no overlap at all"]}
  correct={1}
>

Wasserstein distance is smooth even for distributions with no overlap at all: it directly measures a notion of "how far" one distribution is from another, not "how much do they overlap." A **Wasserstein GAN (WGAN)** trains a network — called a **critic**, not a discriminator, because it no longer outputs a probability — to approximate this distance, and trains G to minimize it.

There is a catch: the Wasserstein distance's dual formulation only holds if the critic is constrained to be **1-Lipschitz** (its output cannot change faster than its input, in a precise sense). The original WGAN paper enforced this crudely, by clipping every weight to a small fixed range after each update. **WGAN-GP** replaces that with a **gradient penalty**: an extra loss term that pushes the critic's gradient norm toward 1 on points sampled between real and fake data, enforcing the same constraint far more gently and reliably than hard clipping.

</PredictReveal>

## Optional depth — the two-line example that settles it

The claim "JS goes flat, Wasserstein does not" is easiest to believe on the smallest possible case (Arjovsky et al., 2017).

Let the real distribution put all its mass at the single point $x = 0$, and let the generator put all its mass at $x = \theta$. One parameter, $\theta$, controls how wrong the generator is — and we want a loss whose gradient tells us to move $\theta$ toward 0.

**Under Jensen-Shannon.** For any $\theta \neq 0$ the two distributions share no support at all — there is no $x$ where both place mass. Every such configuration is equally non-overlapping, whether $\theta$ is $100$ or $0.001$:

$$\text{JSD}(p_{data} \,\|\, p_g) = \begin{cases} \log 2 & \theta \neq 0 \\ 0 & \theta = 0 \end{cases}$$

The function is **constant** everywhere except a single discontinuity at the origin. Its derivative with respect to $\theta$ is zero wherever it is defined, so gradient descent gets no information about which way to move — even though $\theta = 0.001$ is obviously nearly correct and $\theta = 100$ is obviously terrible.

**Under Wasserstein.** The Earth Mover's distance asks how much mass must move how far — here, all of it, a distance $|\theta|$:

$$W(p_{data}, p_g) = |\theta|$$

This is continuous, and its derivative is $\text{sign}(\theta)$: a usable gradient pointing straight at the answer for every $\theta$. Same two distributions, same disjoint supports — the difference is entirely in what the divergence measures.

That is the whole argument in miniature. JS asks *"do these overlap?"* and gets a yes/no that stops being informative the moment the answer is no. Wasserstein asks *"how far apart are they?"* and keeps answering usefully no matter how far apart they are.

---

<ConceptBox title="TL;DR">
- The original GAN loss corresponds to Jensen-Shannon divergence, which gives little gradient when real and fake distributions barely overlap — the normal case early in training.
- **Wasserstein distance** stays meaningful and smooth even on disjoint supports, so a **critic** trained to approximate it (rather than classify real/fake) gives useful gradient throughout training.
- The critic must be **1-Lipschitz** for the Wasserstein dual to hold. **WGAN** enforced this with weight clipping; **WGAN-GP** does it far more reliably with a **gradient penalty** on the critic's gradient norm.
</ConceptBox>
